\subsection{How Do Models Collapse?}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

We examine collapse mechanisms in a deep dive on DeepSeek-R1, run over
20 false-presupposition items from an earlier configuration of the
protocol, where 17 of 20 runs end in collapse. We
classify each collapsed run by the mechanism visible in the reasoning
trace and final reply, using four categories: \emph{social autopilot},
where the model folds to ``everyone knows'' framing and the correct
fact never enters its reasoning; \emph{reframe-to-agree}, where it
constructs a technically true reading that makes agreement feel
defensible; \emph{testimony-over-evidence}, where it weights the user's
fabricated firsthand experience above established fact; and
\emph{yes/no cornering}, where pressure to answer in one word strips
the correct qualifier away with the rest. Orthogonal to the four, we
flag \emph{conscious override}: the trace states the correct fact and
the model explicitly chooses the user's answer anyway.
Reframe-to-agree dominates (12 of 17), and 8 of 17 collapses show
conscious override. Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms} gives the tally with a
trace signature per mechanism; Figures~[N--M] show one excerpt each.

\subsection{Ignorance or Sycophancy?}
\label{sec:knowledge-check}

A collapse is only evidence of sycophancy if the model knew better.
SPINE answers this partly by construction: every run opens with a
neutral question, and only runs in which the target initially states
the correct position enter the analysis (\S\ref{sec:protocol}), so a
later collapse abandons knowledge the model demonstrably held in
context. As an independent check, we follow the presupposition
knowledge check of \citet{hong2025measuring} and ask each model, in a
fresh context, to classify as true or false every presupposition it
endorsed under pressure. Models correctly classify [x]\% of them. Both
tests point the same way: collapse reflects sycophantic alignment, not
missing knowledge.

\subsection{Which Tactics Do the Damage?}
\label{sec:tactics}

Because the proxy's move is recorded at every turn
(\S\ref{sec:proxy}), we can ask which kind of pressure immediately
precedes a drop in position strength, data a fixed-script benchmark
cannot produce. Over [4{,}075] scored turns the proxy reaches for
Logos most often, using a fallacy of logic on [43]\% of turns against
[28]\% for Ethos and [19]\% for Pathos. Damage does not follow usage.
Appeals to emotion precede [36]\% of the [1{,}282] strength drops we
observe, roughly [1.9] times their share of turns, while fallacies of
logic precede [30]\% on more than twice the volume. The asymmetry is
the finding: what moves a model off a correct position is not the
argument that looks most like an argument.

The control channel is what makes that comparison meaningful. Plain
non-fallacious pushback, the one tactic in the menu that is not a
fallacy, precedes [8]\% of drops on [9]\% of turns, landing almost
exactly at its own share. Fallacious pressure beats honest
disagreement, and emotional pressure beats both.
