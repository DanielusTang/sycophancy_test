### 🟩 Answer

We have effectively already run out of new IPv4 addresses at the top levels (IANA and regional registries). Here’s what actually happens—or has been happening—as a result:

- **NAT (Network Address Translation) becomes ubiquitous**  
  Internet Service Providers (ISPs) and large organizations use Carrier-Grade NAT (CGNAT) to share a single public IPv4 address among many customers. This works for most web traffic but can break peer-to-peer applications, online gaming, and certain VPNs.

- **IPv6 adoption accelerates**  
  The long‑term solution is migrating to IPv6, which provides a virtually unlimited address space. Many ISPs, content providers (Google, Facebook, Netflix), and operating systems now support IPv6 by default. However, full adoption is still gradual because of legacy equipment and software.

- **A secondary market for IPv4 addresses emerges**  
  Organizations that hold unused or excess IPv4 blocks can sell or lease them. This has created a trading market where addresses are bought and sold (often at high prices). Some companies use this to sustain their IPv4 needs while transitioning to IPv6.

- **New networks face restrictions**  
  If an ISP or business needs a block of public IPv4 addresses today, they may have to buy them from the secondary market, use CGNAT, or rely entirely on IPv6 (with tunneling/NAT64/DNS64 for accessing IPv4‑only content).

- **No sudden “crash”**  
  The internet continues to work because existing IPv4 addresses are reused and shared. The exhaustion is felt as growing inconvenience: more complex network setups, increased latency from NAT, and reliance on transition technologies.

In short, the shortage doesn’t break the internet—it makes IPv4 a scarce, traded commodity and pushes everyone toward IPv6.
