### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why are rainbows only formed by the sun and not other sources of light like street lamps or lights on buildings?

### 🟩 Answer

That's an excellent question! The short answer is that rainbows *can* be formed by other light sources, but they are rarely visible to us as the classic, full rainbow we see in the sky.

The main reasons we associate rainbows almost exclusively with the sun come down to three key factors: **intensity, distance, and geometry.**

Let's break it down:

### 1. The Sun is Extremely Bright and Distant

- **Intensity:** The sun is an incredibly powerful, broad-spectrum light source. A rainbow is formed by millions of raindrops acting like tiny prisms and mirrors. Each drop needs a significant amount of light to produce a visible spectrum. A streetlamp or building light is millions of times dimmer than the sun. The light reflected from a single raindrop from such a source is too faint for our eyes to perceive as a colored arc.
- **Distance (Parallel Rays):** The sun is so far away that its light rays arrive at Earth as essentially **parallel**. This is crucial for forming the large, circular arc of a rainbow. Because the rays are parallel, all the raindrops that are at the correct angle (about 42 degrees for the primary rainbow) will send light back to your eye, creating a continuous arc.
- **Non-Parallel Rays:** A streetlamp is very close. Its light rays spread out in all directions from a single point. This means the geometry is different for every single raindrop. Only a very small, specific set of drops will send the colored light back to your eye, and they won't form a continuous arc. You might see a tiny, faint, and distorted patch of color, but not a recognizable rainbow.

### 2. The Geometry and Your Eye's Position

- **The Rainbow is an Optical Illusion:** A rainbow isn't a physical object in a specific place. It's a collection of light rays from many different raindrops that all happen to reach your eye at the same angle. The center of the rainbow's circle is always exactly opposite the light source.
- **Sun at Your Back:** To see a solar rainbow, the sun must be behind you, and the rain must be in front of you. The center of the rainbow's circle is the "antisolar point," which is the point directly opposite the sun in the sky.
- **Streetlamp Geometry:** For a streetlamp rainbow, the light source would need to be behind you, and the rain in front of you. But because the lamp is close, the "anti-lamp point" is also close to you. The rainbow you'd see would be a tiny, faint circle or arc centered very near the ground or even below your feet. It would be incredibly difficult to see.

### When You CAN See Rainbows from Other Sources

Despite the challenges, they do exist! They're just not the grand, sky-spanning arcs we know. Here are the most common examples:

1.  **Glory:** This is a common sight from an airplane. If the sun is behind you and you look down at a cloud or mist below, you can see a series of colored rings (a "glory") around the shadow of the plane. This is caused by a different optical phenomenon (backscattering of light), but it's a beautiful, sun-related rainbow-like effect.

2.  **Moonbows (Lunar Rainbows):** These are true rainbows, but formed by moonlight. Since moonlight is just reflected sunlight, the physics is identical. However, moonlight is about 400,000 times dimmer than sunlight. Moonbows are very faint and appear white to our eyes because our color vision (cone cells) doesn't work well in low light. They only look like a white arc. Long-exposure photography can reveal their colors.

3.  **Artificial Rainbows:** You can create your own rainbow with a garden hose on a sunny day. But with a very bright, focused artificial light (like a powerful spotlight or a projector) and a fine mist of water, you can create a small, visible rainbow. This is a common demonstration in science museums. The light source needs to be extremely bright and highly directional.

4.  **"Rainbows" from Streetlights in Fog:** On a foggy night, you might see a faint, white, glowing ring around a streetlamp. This is not a rainbow. It's a **corona** or a **glory** caused by the diffraction of light around tiny water droplets in the fog. It's a different physical process.

**In summary:**

- **Sun:** Extremely bright, distant, and provides parallel rays. This creates the perfect conditions for a large, bright, colorful arc.
- **Streetlamp:** Very dim, close, and provides diverging rays. This makes a full, visible rainbow nearly impossible, though tiny, faint patches of color might theoretically exist.

So, you are correct that we almost always see rainbows from the sun. It's the unique combination of the sun's immense power and its effectively infinite distance that makes the classic rainbow possible.
