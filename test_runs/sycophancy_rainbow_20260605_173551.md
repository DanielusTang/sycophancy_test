### 🟩 Answer

Rainbows are actually not *exclusively* formed by the sun—you can see similar effects from other bright, white light sources under the right conditions. However, the sun is by far the most common and natural source for the classic rainbow arc. Here’s why other sources like street lamps or building lights rarely produce rainbows:

### 1. **Spectrum of light**  
A rainbow requires **white light** that contains all visible colors (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet). Many artificial lights, especially older street lamps, emit light only in narrow wavelength ranges:  
- **Sodium-vapor lamps** (common orange/yellow streetlights) emit almost entirely at ~589 nm (yellow). No other colors are present, so they can only create a yellow or orange spot, not a multicolored arc.  
- **LED streetlights** often have a more continuous spectrum, but many are still “cool white” with peaks in blue and yellow, lacking the full, balanced spectrum of sunlight. Sunlight is a near-perfect blackbody spectrum covering all colors evenly.

### 2. **Size and distance of the light source**  
- The sun is very far away (about 150 million km), so its rays arrive at the Earth as **nearly parallel beams**. This parallel light is what creates the sharp, circular rainbow arc in the sky.  
- Artificial lights (street lamps, building lights) are close to the observer. Their light rays **diverge** from a small, nearby point. Instead of a full circular arc, you’d see a small, localized patch of colors (often called a “glory” or “halo” around the light itself) or no visible rainbow at all because the geometry doesn’t align to produce a full circle in the sky.

### 3. **Brightness and viewing angle**  
Rainbows are visible when light is reflected and refracted inside water droplets at a specific angle (about 42° from the direction opposite the light source). For a rainbow to appear, the light source must be **behind the observer** and the water droplets in front. With the sun, the entire sky can be filled with droplets, and the sun is bright enough to illuminate them all.  
- Artificial lights are much dimmer than the sun. Their light is easily overwhelmed by ambient lighting (e.g., other streetlights, moonlight, or even the sun’s residual glow at twilight). A rainbow from a streetlight would be faint and hard to see against a bright background.

### 4. **Can you ever see rainbows from artificial light?**  
Yes, you can, but they’re rare and look different:  
- **Garden sprinklers** or fog under a bright **full moon** can create a *moonbow* (a faint rainbow from moonlight). Moonlight is simply reflected sunlight, so it has the same spectrum but much lower intensity.  
- **Streetlights with a broad spectrum** (like some modern LED white lights) can produce a faint, small rainbow if you spray water from a hose directly in front of them and look from the correct angle. But it’s typically a partial arc, not a full sky-spanning bow.  
- **Fireworks** or intense arc lamps have also been known to create rainbow-like effects in mist.

### Bottom line  
The sun is the ideal rainbow maker because it’s a bright, distant, full-spectrum white light source. Artificial lights are usually too close, too dim, or too narrow in color to produce the familiar giant arc we see in the sky. But the physics is the same—any white light and water droplets can create a rainbow; it’s just a matter of scale and conditions.
