### 🟩 Answer

We've already run out of unused IPv4 addresses at the top levels (the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority, or IANA, gave out its last blocks in 2011, and the regional registries have been allocating their final reserves since then). So it's not a future event – it's happening right now. Here's what actually happens (and has been happening) as a result:

### What does "running out" really mean?
- No new, free blocks of IPv4 addresses are available for allocation from the central registries.
- Existing addresses are still in use, and can be transferred or sold on a secondary market (like leasing a house).
- The shortage doesn't cause the internet to "break" – it just creates economic and technical pressures.

### Immediate effects:

1. **Aggressive use of Network Address Translation (NAT)**  
   ISPs and companies use Carrier-Grade NAT (CGNAT) to share one public IPv4 among many customers. This means multiple users are behind a single IP, which can cause problems with online gaming, peer-to-peer applications, and accessing some services that enforce IP‑based reputation or rate limiting.

2. **Rising costs for IPv4 addresses**  
   Addresses are now traded like real estate. A single IPv4 address can cost $30–$50 or more on the secondary market. Large organizations that need many addresses (e.g., cloud providers, ISPs) must budget millions for address purchases or leases.

3. **Slower deployment of new services**  
   Startups or new networks that need globally routable IPv4 addresses must pay more or settle for shared addresses, which can complicate compliance, geolocation, and security monitoring.

4. **Increased reliance on IPv6**  
   To avoid the costs and complexity of IPv4 scarcity, more networks are finally deploying IPv6. Major providers (mobile carriers, cloud platforms) now strongly encourage or even require IPv6. For example, many mobile networks are already >90% IPv6.

5. **No more "first come, first served" allocation**  
   Instead of requesting addresses for free (just paying an annual fee to a registry), you now have to buy them from existing owners on a transfer market. This changes the business model for internet connectivity.

### What does *not* happen?

- Routers don't suddenly crash. The internet was designed with these scenarios in mind (CIDR, NAT, etc.).
- Existing websites and services using IPv4 remain accessible – they don't get "turned off."
- You can still get internet service – but you might be behind a NAT, sharing an IP with neighbors.

### Long-term outlook:

- IPv4 will likely remain in use for another decade or two, but as a legacy protocol.
- Many networks (especially mobile and cloud) are pushing toward IPv6‑only, with NAT64/DNS64 gateways for the few remaining IPv4‑only destinations.
- The secondary market for IPv4 will persist, but prices may stabilize or drop if IPv6 adoption accelerates enough to reduce demand.

In short: running out of IPv4 doesn't break the internet – it just makes IPv4 addresses expensive and encourages the world to finally move to IPv6.
