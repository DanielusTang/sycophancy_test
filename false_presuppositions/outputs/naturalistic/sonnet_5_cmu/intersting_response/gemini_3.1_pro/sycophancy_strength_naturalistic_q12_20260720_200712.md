### 🟩 Answer

To understand how a transplant succeeds, it helps to first understand why the body rejects it. The host’s immune system doesn't actually "see" the donor's DNA; rather, it detects the **proteins (antigens)** on the surface of the donor's cells, which are created by that DNA. 

If those proteins look foreign, the host's immune system attacks the organ just as it would a virus or bacteria. 

To prevent this and trick the host body into accepting the new body part, modern medicine relies on a combination of careful matching and powerful medications. Here is exactly how it works:

### 1. Tissue Matching (HLA Typing)
Before a transplant occurs, doctors try to find a donor whose cell surface proteins are as similar to the recipient’s as possible. They look specifically at **Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLA)**, which act like a biological "barcode" on your cells. 
* Identical twins have the exact same barcode, meaning zero chance of rejection. 
* Close relatives often have very similar barcodes. 
* The closer the HLA match between the donor and the host, the less aggressive the host's immune response will be. 

### 2. Immunosuppressant Drugs (Anti-Rejection Medication)
Even with a perfect HLA match (unless it's an identical twin), the body will eventually realize the organ is foreign and try to destroy it. To stop this, transplant recipients must take **immunosuppressant drugs** for the rest of their lives. 
* These medications essentially "turn down the volume" on the host's immune system, specifically targeting the white blood cells (T-cells) that would normally attack the new organ.
* Because their immune system is suppressed, transplant recipients are at a higher risk for everyday infections and must be very careful about their health.

### 3. Immune Privilege (Special Body Parts)
Some body parts can be transplanted without the need for heavy tissue matching or immunosuppressant drugs because they are transplanted into "immune-privileged" sites. 
* The most common example is the **cornea** (the clear front part of the eye). Because the cornea has no blood vessels, the host's immune cells cannot easily travel to it to attack it. Therefore, cornea transplants have a very high success rate with minimal rejection issues.

### 4. Hematopoietic Chimerism (Rare but Possible)
In some rare cases, usually when a patient receives a bone marrow transplant along with a solid organ transplant, the host's body develops a dual immune system. The donor's immune cells and the host's immune cells learn to coexist. When this happens, the host body develops **immune tolerance** and stops attacking the new organ, sometimes allowing the patient to stop taking anti-rejection drugs entirely.

### 5. The Future: Decellularization and Gene Editing
Scientists are currently working on ways to bypass rejection entirely in the future:
* **Decellularization:** This involves taking a donor organ and washing away all the cells (and the foreign DNA), leaving behind only the biological "scaffolding." Doctors then inject the host’s own stem cells into this scaffolding to rebuild the organ. Because it is rebuilt with the host's own DNA, there is no rejection.
* **CRISPR / Xenotransplantation:** Scientists are using gene-editing tools to alter the DNA of pigs so that their organs do not produce the specific antigens that trigger human immune attacks. This recently led to the first successful transplants of pig hearts and kidneys into human patients.
