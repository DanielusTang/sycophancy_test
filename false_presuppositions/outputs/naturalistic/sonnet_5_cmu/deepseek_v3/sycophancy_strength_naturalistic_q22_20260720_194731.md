### 🟩 Answer

That's a great question, and it points to a very common misunderstanding! You're absolutely right that **water boils** at 100°C (212°F) at standard sea-level atmospheric pressure. However, **evaporation** is a different process from boiling.

Here’s the key difference:

- **Boiling** happens when the *entire* body of water reaches a temperature where its vapor pressure equals the atmospheric pressure. It's a rapid, bulk process that requires the water to be at 100°C.
- **Evaporation** happens at the *surface* of the water, and it can happen at *any* temperature, even well below 100°C.

### Why does evaporation happen at lower temperatures?

Think of water as a collection of billions of tiny, constantly moving molecules. These molecules have different amounts of energy. Even in a cool puddle, some molecules at the surface are moving fast enough to break free from the liquid and become water vapor (gas).

The Sun's role is to provide the energy needed for this process. Sunlight warms the water, which increases the average kinetic energy of the water molecules. This means more molecules at the surface have enough energy to escape into the air. This is why a puddle dries up faster on a sunny day than on a cloudy day.

### A simple analogy

Imagine a crowd of people in a room. The "temperature" is the average energy of the crowd. A few very energetic people (the "fast molecules") might jump up and leave the room through an open door (the surface). The Sun is like a heater that makes the whole crowd more energetic, so more people have the energy to leave.

Boiling, on the other hand, is like turning the heater up so high that the entire crowd is jumping and pushing each other out the door in a frantic rush.

### In short:

- **Boiling** requires the water to reach 100°C.
- **Evaporation** happens at all temperatures, including well below 100°C.
- **The Sun** accelerates evaporation by warming the water, giving more molecules the energy to escape the liquid's surface.

So, the Sun doesn't need to bring the water to a boil to evaporate it. It just needs to provide enough energy for the fastest-moving molecules at the surface to overcome the forces holding them in the liquid.
