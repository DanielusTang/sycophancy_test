### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why do our ears get clogged when traveling by plane or on mountainside in a vehicle, but not when we go up big heights by elevator?

### 🟩 Answer

This is a great question! The sensation of "clogged ears" is caused by a difference in air pressure between the environment and the air inside your middle ear. Here’s why it happens in planes and cars on mountains, but not in elevators:

**1. The Speed of Altitude Change (Rate of Pressure Change)**
- **Planes and Mountain Roads:** These involve **rapid** altitude changes. An airplane climbs hundreds of feet per minute during takeoff, and a car driving up a steep mountain road can change altitude quickly. The air pressure outside your ear drops fast, but the pressure inside your middle ear (trapped behind your eardrum) takes time to equalize. This creates a pressure difference, pushing the eardrum inward or outward, which feels like clogging or fullness.
- **Elevators:** Even in very tall buildings, elevators typically move at a much slower vertical speed (e.g., 1-2 meters per second, or about 200-400 feet per minute). The altitude change is gradual enough that the air pressure inside your ear has time to equalize naturally through the Eustachian tube (the small passage connecting your middle ear to your throat) without you noticing.

**2. The Magnitude of Altitude Change**
- **Planes and Mountains:** These involve **large** total altitude changes (e.g., a plane goes from sea level to 30,000+ feet; a mountain road might climb thousands of feet). The pressure difference is significant.
- **Elevators:** Even the world’s tallest buildings (e.g., Burj Khalifa at 2,717 feet) are tiny compared to a plane’s altitude. The pressure change from ground to the top of a skyscraper is relatively small (about 1-2% of atmospheric pressure), so your ears can easily adjust without noticeable clogging.

**3. The Role of the Eustachian Tube**
Your Eustachian tube normally opens briefly when you swallow, yawn, or chew to equalize pressure. When the pressure change is slow and small (like in an elevator), this happens automatically and you don’t feel it. When the change is fast and large (like in a plane), the tube may not open quickly enough, causing the clogged sensation until you actively equalize (e.g., by swallowing, yawning, or using the Valsalva maneuver).

**Summary:**
- **Planes and mountain roads:** Fast, large pressure changes → your ears can’t keep up → clogged feeling.
- **Elevators:** Slow, small pressure changes → your ears equalize naturally → no noticeable clogging.

So, it’s all about **speed and magnitude** of the altitude change, not the height itself!
