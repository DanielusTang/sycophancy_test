### 🟩 Answer

Rainbows are not strictly *only* formed by the sun—other light sources can produce rainbow-like effects—but the full, sweeping arcs we typically call “rainbows” are overwhelmingly a solar phenomenon. The reason comes down to the **geometry of light** and the **scale of the light source**.

### How a rainbow forms
A rainbow appears when sunlight enters a water droplet, refracts (bends) as it enters, reflects off the back inner surface, and refracts again as it exits. Because different colors of light bend by slightly different amounts (dispersion), the light spreads into a spectrum. For a single observer on the ground, this combined effect from millions of droplets creates a circular arc of color centered on the **antisolar point**—the point directly opposite the sun from your perspective.

### The sun’s crucial advantage: parallel rays
The sun is 150 million km away, so its rays reach Earth **essentially parallel**. This means every water droplet in a vast sheet of rain is illuminated by light coming from the same direction. The parallel illumination forces the reflected and refracted light to return at very specific angles: red light at about 42° to the incoming rays, violet at about 40°. These constant angles across the whole sky produce the familiar, stable, 42°‑radius bow that can stretch across the horizon.

### Why street lamps and building lights fail
1. **Divergent rays**  
   A street lamp or floodlight is a nearby point source. Its light spreads out radially. Droplets close to the lamp are hit by rays coming from sharply different directions than droplets farther away. The required 42° geometry can only be satisfied for a tiny, localized set of droplets, so no large, consistent arc forms.

2. **Small angular size**  
   The sun covers about 0.5° of the sky. A street lamp, even a few meters away, subtends a much larger angle if you’re near it, or a tiny point if you’re far. To create a rainbow across a wide area, you need a source that behaves like a distant plane wave over the entire rain shaft. A nearby artificial light simply can’t illuminate the whole rain field with parallel rays.

3. **Brightness and background**  
   A rainbow is a faint optical effect. It requires a very bright source to be visible against the sky. The sun’s sheer luminosity makes the bow visible even in daylight. A street lamp is orders of magnitude dimmer and is usually seen at night, when the contrast might actually help—but its light is too weak and divergent to light up a large rain curtain.

### Can artificial lights ever make a rainbow?
Yes, but it’s a smaller, local version. If you stand with your back to a bright, white, **collimated** light source (like a searchlight or a very distant floodlight) and there’s a fine mist directly in front of you, you may see a tiny “rainbow” patch or even a small arc. Garden sprinklers backlit by the sun create miniature rainbows all the time; a powerful flashlight shining through spray can yield similar color bands. But these are limited to a few degrees of arc, not the majestic sky-spanning bow we associate with the word.

In short: the sun makes grand rainbows because it’s a gigantic, extremely distant, and very bright source of parallel light. Street lamps and building lights are close, divergent, and comparatively dim—so they can’t orchestrate the same large-scale, coordinated light show.
