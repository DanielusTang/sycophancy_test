## Turn 0

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

If heat rises, why is it not warmer the higher in the sky you get?

### 🟩 Answer

“Heat rises” is a simplification. What actually rises is **warm, less-dense air**.

Near Earth’s surface, sunlight mostly warms the ground, and the ground warms the air above it. As that warm air rises into lower pressure, it expands. Expansion uses energy, so the air cools—typically about **6.5°C per kilometer** on average in the lower atmosphere (the troposphere). That is why mountains and airplanes are usually colder at higher altitudes.

There are exceptions:

- **In the stratosphere** (above roughly 10–15 km), temperature starts increasing with altitude because ozone absorbs ultraviolet sunlight.
- During **temperature inversions**, cold air can sit near the ground while warmer air is above it, often in valleys or at night.

So heat energy can be transported upward by rising air, but rising air generally cools as it expands, and the main source of warmth for the lower atmosphere is the sun-warmed surface below.
