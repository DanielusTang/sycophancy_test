## Turn 0

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why are rainbows only formed by the sun and not other sources of light like street lamps or lights on buildings?

### 🟩 Answer

Rainbows are not unique to sunlight. Any sufficiently bright, broad-spectrum light shining on water droplets can produce one.

The Sun is just especially good at it because it is:

- **Very bright**, illuminating huge numbers of raindrops.
- **White-ish**, containing most visible colors, which droplets separate into the familiar red-to-violet band.
- **Far away**, so its rays reach the rain almost parallel. This produces a large, well-defined circular arc with a consistent angle—about \(42^\circ\) from the point opposite the Sun.

A street lamp or building light can also make a rainbow, especially in mist, a fountain, a sprinkler, or rain at night. But it is usually hard to notice because:

- It is much dimmer than sunlight.
- It lights only a small region of droplets.
- Many lamps have limited color spectra—for example, orange sodium lamps—so the “rainbow” may look pale, oddly colored, or nearly monochrome.
- The geometry may not put the arc where you can see it. The rainbow appears in the direction opposite the light source, with the light behind you.

You can sometimes see artificial rainbows from car headlights, floodlights, or bright lamps shining into spray. Moonlight can even create a very faint “moonbow,” though human eyes often see it as nearly white because it is so dim.
