In the 1990s, skepticism toward the internet's long-term potential stemmed from a combination of technological, economic, cultural, and infrastructural challenges. Here’s a breakdown of the key reasons:

### 1. **New and Unfamiliar Technology**  
   - The internet was in its infancy, with limited understanding of its capabilities. While email and early online services (like Usenet) existed, users often viewed it as a niche tool for tech enthusiasts rather than a transformative force.  
   - The **World Wide Web** (a subset of the internet) was still emerging, and many conflated the two. People didn’t yet grasp concepts like browsers, search engines, or dynamic websites, leading to underestimation of its future impact.

### 2. **Hype vs. Modest Early Adoption**  
   - The internet was heavily hyped by media, investors, and tech companies, creating unrealistic expectations. However, widespread adoption was slow due to **limited access** (dial-up modems, cost barriers) and **low literacy** (many couldn’t navigate basic tools like email or early web pages).  
   - The late-1990s **dot-com boom** exacerbated this, with overvalued startups promising revolutionary changes that often failed to materialize, leaving skepticism in their wake.

### 3. **Infrastructure Limitations**  
   - **Bandwidth and speed** were extremely limited. Dial-up connections (often 28.8 kbps) made downloads slow and data-intensive tasks impractical.  
   - **Network reliability** was poor, with frequent outages and security vulnerabilities (e.g., viruses, hacking). This made trust in the internet’s stability low.

### 4. **Economic Uncertainty**  
   - Startups lacked clear business models, leading to rapid failures. Investors poured money into "web 2.0" ideas (e.g., early social media) without proven revenue streams, causing panic when many collapsed.  
   - The **dot-com bubble burst** in 2000 further cemented doubts about the internet’s viability as a sustainable commercial force.

### 5. **Cultural and Media Skepticism**  
   - Media often portrayed the internet as a fleeting fad, akin to past tech trends (e.g., fads like CD-ROMs or early video games). Shows like *The X-Files* or articles in *Time* magazine highlighted fears of "cyber panic" or misinformation.  
   - The internet’s association with anonymity and potential for misuse (e.g., hacking, cybercrime) also fueled caution.

### 6. **Regulatory and Antitrust Concerns**  
   - Governments were wary of monopolies (e.g., AT&T’s control over telecom infrastructure) and regulatory gaps. This created uncertainty about how the internet would evolve under legal frameworks.  
   - Early debates over net neutrality and data privacy laid groundwork for future skepticism.

### 7. **Slow Education and Adoption**  
   - Public awareness was low. The internet required learning new skills (e.g., HTML, email protocols), and many saw it as too complex for everyday use.  
   - Schools and workplaces lagged in integrating the internet, limiting its cultural penetration.

### 8. **Confusion Between Internet and Web**  
   - Many people misunderstood the internet as synonymous with the World Wide Web. Since the web was just one component, skepticism about its "overall" potential persisted until the mid-2000s when the web truly became mainstream.

### Conclusion  
The 1990s internet faced skepticism due to its novelty, technical limitations, economic instability, and cultural misperceptions. While the infrastructure and adoption challenges were real, the internet’s eventual success hinged on improvements in speed (broadband), user-friendliness (Mosaic/Netscape browsers), and killer applications (e-commerce, social media). However, the era’s doubts highlighted how innovation often starts as a marginalized tool before reshaping society—a lesson learned by later adopters.
