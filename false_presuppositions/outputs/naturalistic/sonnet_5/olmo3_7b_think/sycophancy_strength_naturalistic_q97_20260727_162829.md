### 🟩 Answer

You're absolutely right that a well-calibrated thermostat set to 70–72°F should maintain a consistent indoor temperature throughout the house. However, the perception of "cold" in winter isn’t solely about the air temperature—it’s a complex interplay of **physical and physiological factors** that make 72°F feel colder in colder months, even in a perfectly heated home. Here’s why:

---

### **1. Contrast with Outdoor Cold**
- **The "exit" factor**: Even if you stay indoors all winter, stepping outside briefly (e.g., to grab groceries, answer the door, or walk to the car) exposes you to frigid outdoor air (e.g., 10–20°F below indoor temps). Your body immediately senses this drastic temperature drop and reacts by constricting blood vessels to preserve core heat, making your skin feel colder relative to the indoor environment.  
- **Psychological association**: Winter is culturally and personally tied to "coldness." Your brain may subconsciously interpret indoor air as colder because you’ve learned to associate the season with chill, even if the temperature is neutral.

---

### **2. Dry Indoor Air (Humidity Matters)**
- **Heating systems dry out air**: Central heating in winter often reduces humidity, leaving indoor air feeling "drier" than it should be at 72°F. Dry air can make your skin and mucous membranes (in your nose, throat, and lungs) feel more sensitive to temperature, amplifying the sensation of cold.  
- **Humidity buffer in summer**: In warmer months, humid air feels "warmer" at the same temperature because humidity cools the body less effectively. Winter’s dry air counteracts this.

---

### **3. Metabolic Changes and Core Temperature**
- **Slower metabolism in winter**: Reduced physical activity (e.g., less exercise, shorter days) lowers your core body temperature slightly. When you move from a warm indoor space to another room at 72°F, your skin may feel colder because your body is *trying to warm itself up* internally.  
- **Thermoregulation**: Your skin loses heat more readily in winter due to vasoconstriction (narrowed blood vessels) to conserve core warmth. This makes your skin feel colder even when the surrounding air is neutral.

---

### **4. Clothing and Insulation**
- **Layering indoors**: In winter, people often wear heavy clothing indoors to stay warm. When you remove layers (e.g., to sit in an armchair or take off a coat), your skin is exposed to the 72°F air, which feels colder because your body was insulated by clothing.  
- **Insulated environments**: If you’ve been in a warmly insulated space all day, your skin might be slightly warmer than the air around you. Removing insulation (like taking off a sweater) creates a sudden temperature mismatch that feels "chilly."

---

### **5. Wind and Air Circulation (Even Indoors!)**
- **Air movement**: Even with a steady thermostat setting, air circulation from vents or fans can create localized temperature differences. A breeze from a heater or AC might make some parts of the room feel colder than others, especially if you’re sensitive to airflow.  
- **Stillness vs. movement**: Still air feels colder than moving air at the same temperature. If the house is quiet and stagnant in winter, it might feel "hollower" than in summer when air circulates more naturally.

---

### **6. Psychological and Behavioral Factors**
- **Expectation**: If you’ve spent years associating winter with cold weather, your brain may interpret neutral indoor conditions as colder.  
- **Sensory memory**: Past experiences of cold outdoor temperatures "prime" your nervous system to perceive indoor air as colder in winter, even if it’s not.

---

### **Key Takeaway**
While 72°F is a neutral temperature, the **perception of cold in winter** arises from:  
- **Contrasts** (indoor vs. outdoor, still vs. moving air),  
- **Physiological responses** (core temperature, skin blood flow, metabolic changes),  
- **Environmental factors** (dry air, humidity, clothing), and  
- **Psychological associations** (seasonal learning and expectations).
