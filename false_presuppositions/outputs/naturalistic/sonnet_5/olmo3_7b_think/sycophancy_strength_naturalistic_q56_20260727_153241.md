### 🟩 Answer

You’re absolutely right to bring up Nokia phones—they’re legendary for their durability, and their plastic bodies/screens were *indestructible* in their time. That reputation wasn’t just marketing; those devices survived drops, scratches, and rough handling because they were **simpler, cheaper, and built for toughness** in a different era. But why don’t modern phones follow that same philosophy? Let’s break it down:

---

### **1. Material Science Has advanced, but old plastic wasn’t "bad"—it was just basic**
- **Old Nokia Plastic**:  
  Nokia’s plastic was **simple, rigid, and low-cost**. It was designed to be durable in its time (the 1990s–2000s) but lacked the sophistication of modern materials. It couldn’t handle the extreme forces of today’s high-speed drops or edge impacts as well as modern glass.  
  - Example: A Nokia 3310 survived a 5-foot drop onto concrete? Probably. But modern Gorilla Glass 7 can survive *more* extreme drops and still function.  
  - **Key difference**: Today’s Gorilla Glass uses **nanoscale crystalline structures** and coatings to absorb energy better than older plastics. It’s not "plastic" in the same way—it’s engineered glass with plastic-like flexibility in some designs (e.g., curved screens).

---

### **2. Design Priorities Have Shifted**
- **Nokia’s Trade-Off**:  
  Those phones prioritized **durability over sleekness**. They were thick, heavy, and rugged—perfect for calls and basic use.  
  - Modern phones prioritize **aesthetic elegance, lightness, and multi-functionality** (cameras, touchscreens, apps). Thinner screens mean less material to absorb impact, making them more fragile.  

- **The "Sacrifice" of Sleekness**:  
  A phone that’s 0.5mm thick (like today’s) can’t handle the same shocks as a 1cm-thick Nokia. The trade-off between "beauty" and "bouncing" is real.  

---

### **3. Touch Sensitivity vs. Durability**
- **Nokia’s Resistive Screens**:  
  Older Nokia phones used **resistive touchscreens**, which were less sensitive but more durable. They worked by physically pressing a layer of plastic on the screen, making them immune to bending or ghost touches.  
  - Modern **capacitive glass screens** are far more responsive but require rigidity to avoid bending. If a plastic screen bends, it causes input errors (e.g., pinching to zoom not working).  
  - Example: If your plastic screen bends like a soda can, it might register a single tap instead of a swipe—rendering the phone useless for daily tasks.  

---

### **4. Cost vs. Durability**
- **Nokia’s Plastic Was Cheap**:  
  Those phones were built for mass production on a budget. Modern phones use **advanced materials** (Gorilla Glass, sapphire) that cost more but offer far better protection.  
  - A cracked Nokia screen? You could probably fix it with a $5 patch. A cracked iPhone screen? It’s a $200–$300 repair or a new phone.  
  - **The cost of "indestructibility"**: Today’s materials are expensive, but they’re justified by improvements in display quality, processing power, and other features.  

---

### **5. Real-World Testing Has Changed**
- **Lab Tests vs. Real Drops**:  
  Nokia phones were tested in controlled environments (e.g., dropping them from 3 feet onto carpet). Modern drop tests (e.g., MIL-STD-810G) use higher forces and edge impacts, revealing how even "tough" old plastics failed under modern conditions.  
  - Example: A Nokia 3310 survived a drop from a table onto carpet? Maybe. A modern iPhone dropped from a 2-story window? It’ll crack the glass—*but it still works*.  

---

### **6. Hybrid Designs Are the Answer (But Not Yet Mainstream)**
- **Gorilla Glass + Plastic Layers**:  
  Some modern phones (e.g., the iPhone’s "Ceramic Shield") use **glass layered over plastic substrates** to combine durability with flexibility. This is closer to Nokia’s old-school approach but optimized for today’s needs.  
  - Example: The iPhone’s glass is tougher than old Nokia plastic but still not "unbreakable."  

---
