---

### **1. Why Do Ears "Pop" if Pressure is the Same Inside and Out?**
The key here is that **"equal pressure" doesn’t mean static pressure everywhere**. Your ears experience **transient pressure changes** due to:
- **Airflow over the outer ear**: As the plane moves through the air (even at cruising altitude), air flows past your ears, creating tiny pressure fluctuations in the ear canal.  
  - Imagine blowing air into a balloon: the pressure inside the balloon changes slightly as you blow. Similarly, your ear canal experiences these micro-changes as the plane’s airspeed changes (e.g., during turbulence, acceleration, or even gentle yawning/swallowing).  
- **Eustachian Tube Function**: Your Eustachian tubes (tiny passages connecting your middle ear to your throat) automatically open and close to **equalize pressure** in your ear. This is why you hear a *pop* or *click* when they open (like when you yawn on the ground).  
  - Even at constant cabin pressure, these tubes keep working to balance the slight pressure shifts caused by movement or airflow.  

**In short**: The ears "pop" because of **airflow and movement**, not because the cabin pressure is actually changing. The Eustachian system is just doing its job to keep your inner ear comfortable!

---

### **2. Why Is the Cabin Air Dry?**
You’re correct that cabin pressure is constant, but that doesn’t explain the dryness. Here’s why:
- **High-Altitude Air is Naturally Drier**:  
  At 35,000 feet (about 10,600 meters), the air is **much colder** and **less dense** than at sea level. Cold air holds **less moisture** (relative humidity drops with temperature). Even if the cabin is pressurized to sea-level conditions, the outside air (which the cabin air is drawn from) is inherently drier at high altitude.  
  - Think of it like this: If you took a humid room and cooled it to freezing, the humidity would drop. The cabin’s air is "recreated" from this already dry high-altitude air.  
- **Cabin Humidity Systems**:

---

### **3. A Critical Nuance: Absolute vs. Relative Pressure**
- **Absolute pressure** (the actual force of air molecules pressing on your eardrum) is the same inside and outside the plane.  
- **Relative pressure changes** (differences caused by movement or airflow) still happen in your ear canal. Your body interprets these as "pressure imbalances" and responds by opening the Eustachian tubes.

### **What’s Normal vs. What’s Not?**
