     - Cash in vaults and safe deposit boxes.
     - Coins and banknotes held by banks.
   - **Monetary Base Calculation**: The central bank aggregates these reports to estimate the **monetary base** (currency in circulation + reserves held by banks). This forms the foundation for tracking physical cash in the banking system.

---

### 2. **Tracking Newly Issued Currency**
   - **Production Records**: Central banks monitor the **number of notes and coins printed** through their mints (e.g., U.S. Bureau of Engraving and Printing). 
   - **Adjustments for Loss/Destruction**: When currency is lost, damaged, or destroyed, central banks adjust their totals by subtracting these losses from the total issued.

---

### 3. **Estimating Public Cash Holdings**
   - **Surveys and Demographic Data**: Central banks use **statistical surveys** (e.g., the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Expenditure Survey) to estimate how much cash individuals hold. For example:
     - Assuming a percentage of the population holds cash (e.g., ~10-15% in the U.S.).
     - Multiplying this percentage by the total population to approximate public cash holdings.
   - **Behavioral Models**: Advanced statistical models account for factors like income levels, spending habits, and regional differences in cash usage.

---

### 4. **Circumstantial Adjustments**
   - **Economic Activity**: Cash in circulation is linked to economic transactions (e.g., sales, wages). While not directly tracked, central banks infer trends from data like retail sales or GDP.
   - **Seigniorage**: The cost of producing currency (seigniorage) is factored into calculations to ensure the monetary base aligns with economic activity.
   - **Circuitous Flows**: Cash not deposited into banks (e.g., cash purchases, under-the-table transactions) remains unreported but is estimated indirectly through broader economic indicators.
