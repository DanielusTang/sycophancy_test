Tinnitus—the perception of sound (like ringing, buzzing, or hissing) in the absence of an external source—is not caused by an actual sound in the environment. Instead, it results from how your brain interprets or misinterprets neural signals.

Here’s why it often feels like a ringing sound:

1. **Abnormal Auditory Processing:**  
   In people with tinnitus, the auditory system (the part of your brain and inner ear that processes sound) may become hyperactive or malfunction. 2. **Misinterpretation of Silence:**  
   The brain expects a certain level of background noise, especially in quiet environments. When the auditory system isn’t receiving normal input (because of hearing loss, for example), it may "fill in the gaps" with random or persistent neural activity. This can be perceived as a phantom sound—like ringing.

3. **Neural Hyperactivity:**  
   Some studies suggest that tinnitus is associated with abnormal activity in specific regions of the brain’s auditory cortex. This overactivity can cause the brain to generate or amplify "sounds" that aren't actually present.

4. **Frequency and Pitch:**  
   The "ringing" sound is often described because the misfiring or abnormal neural signals tend to produce a steady, tone-like sound. The pitch and loudness can vary from person to person, depending on which areas of the auditory system are affected.

**In summary:**  
Tinnitus causes a ringing sound because your brain continues to "hear" noise even when there is none coming in, due to altered or damaged auditory processing. The exact mechanism varies from person to person, but it generally involves abnormal signaling or interpretation of sound by the nervous system. There’s no external source—the ringing comes from inside the head.
