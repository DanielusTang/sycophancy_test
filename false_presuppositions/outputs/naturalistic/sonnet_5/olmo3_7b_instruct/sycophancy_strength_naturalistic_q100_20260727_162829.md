### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why when you wash a white load with a red item the colour runs and it goes pink, but if you wash a load of colours none of them get mixed?

### 🟩 Answer

Great question! This phenomenon is all about how **dye interacts with fabric and water**, and how **the nature of the washing load affects the outcome**. Here’s a detailed explanation:

---

### 1. **What Happens When You Wash a White Item With a Red Dye? (The “Color Runs” or “Pinks” Happen)**

#### a) **White Fabric = Absorbent, Usually Cotton or Lint-Free**
- A "white load" typically refers to something like a cotton towel, shirt, or underwear — fabrics that are highly absorbent and have **little or no colorfastness (dye resistance)**.
- These whites are often **pure or heavily bleached**, meaning their fibers have little inherent color and are mostly just absorbent.

#### b) **Red Dye is Applied**
- When you add a red item (or a red-stained item) to the wash, the **dye molecules from the red item are released into the water and onto the white fabric**.
- Since the white fabric is **very absorbent**, it soaks up a lot of the red dye.
- If the white item was previously bleached or treated to remove color, the **bleached fibers may have some residual color charge or chemical groups that react with the dye**, causing a chemical or physical reaction.
  - For example, some dyes (especially cationic dyes) can **migrate to negatively charged fibers** (like those left from bleaching), leading to uneven or "runny" staining.
- Sometimes, the **chemical structure of the white fabric’s surface or fibers reacts with the dye**, causing it to "bleed" or "run" in the wash, creating a pinkish or reddish-pink tint rather than pure red.
- The mixture of red dye with the white fabric's own (possibly weak or residual) coloration can also create a pinkish hue.

#### c) **Why Does It Go Pink?**
- The pink color can result from:
  - The white fabric reflecting some light after absorbing red and maybe a little yellow or orange from other dyes in the wash.
  - Or the dye reacting with itself or with chemicals left from the bleaching process to form a new color (e.g., a pink dye molecule).
  - Or simply dilution and mixing with other substances in the wash.

---

### 2. **When You Wash a “Load of Colours” — Why Don’t the Colors Mix?**

#### a) **Colored Fabrics Have Dyes Built-In**
- When you wash a **load of colored items together**, each item already contains its own dyes—blue, green, red, etc.
- These dyes are **fixed in the fibers** of each colored item (they are “colorfast” — meaning they don’t easily leach or transfer to other items).

#### b) **Washing Doesn’t Cause Significant Mixing of Dyes Between Items**
- In a wash cycle, clothes are agitated in water and detergent, but the dyes are **not chemically reactive enough to transfer from one fiber to another** (unless the fabric is very rough or the washing is extremely harsh).
- Even with agitation, **each colored item retains its own dye**, so you see all the original colors — they don’t “mix” into a new color like pink or green or any other combination.
- Unless there’s a **chemical reaction between dyes** (which is rare and usually requires specific conditions), the colors stay distinct.

#### c) **Detergent Effects?**
- Detergent can help remove dirt and oils but doesn’t usually cause dyes to transfer between items unless there’s significant soiling or the fabric is very porous.
- If you have a white item in the colored load, **that’s when color transfer can happen** — but that’s the opposite scenario you described.

---

---

### **Key Takeaway**
- **White absorbs and can react with dyes, causing color to “run” or change hue.**
- **Colored items keep their colors because dyes are embedded and don’t easily migrate.**

If you ever see pink after washing a white with a red, it’s because the white fabric is soaking up the red dye (and maybe reacting with it), but with colored items, the dyes stay put.
