### 1. **High Operating Costs**
- **Fuel Consumption:** Flying at supersonic speeds (Mach 2 or higher) dramatically increases fuel consumption. The Concorde, for example, burned about 4,000 gallons of fuel per hour, which was extremely expensive compared to subsonic jets.
- **Maintenance & Engineering:** The advanced technology required for supersonic flight meant more complex and expensive maintenance, with fewer parts available and higher repair costs.

### 2. **Environmental Concerns**
- **Noise Pollution:** Supersonic aircraft produce a distinctive, loud “sonic boom” as they break the sound barrier, causing significant noise over large areas. This led to strict noise regulations and limited where Concorde could fly (mainly over water).
- **Emissions:** While emissions per passenger were lower than older jet engines (due to higher altitude and more efficient engines), supersonic aircraft still had a larger carbon footprint per passenger-mile compared to subsonic flights.

### 3. **Regulatory and Political Barriers**
- **International Airspace Restrictions:** The airspace over populated regions (like much of North America and Europe) was off-limits to supersonic aircraft due to the disruptive nature of their sonic booms. This limited routes and made scheduling and operations complicated.
- **Safety Regulations:** Aviation authorities (like the FAA and ECAA) were reluctant to approve new supersonic commercial jets due to the unproven safety records and the risks associated with breaking the sound barrier.
- **Terrorism Concerns:** After events like the 9/11 attacks, there was increased scrutiny on any aircraft capable of rapid transcontinental travel, further deterring investment in supersonic technology.

### 4. **Economic Infeasibility**
- **Low Passenger Numbers:** Even with demand initially high (especially from business travelers), the economics were difficult. The Concorde typically had very few passengers per flight due to high ticket prices and operational costs.
- **Competition:** The rise of low-cost and efficient subsonic jets (like Boeing 737, Airbus A320, and later long-haul jets like the 777 and 787) made supersonic flights uncompetitive in terms of price and convenience.
- **No Demand for New Supersonic Aircraft:** After the Concorde’s retirement in 2003, there was little commercial demand for new supersonic airliners, and major companies (Boeing, Airbus, Lockheed Martin) shifted focus to other projects.

### 5. **Technical Limitations and Risks**
- **Aging Technology:** The Concorde was designed in the 1960s-70s, and by the 2000s, it was showing its age with maintenance issues and safety concerns.
- **Lack of Modern Features:** It lacked modern avionics, pressurization systems, and safety features common in newer jets.
- **Safety Records:** Although the Concorde had no major accidents, the potential risks (especially from structural failures or engine issues at high speeds) were seen as too high for further development.

---

### In Summary
**Supersonic passenger flights like the Concorde were abolished because they were too expensive to operate, posed significant environmental and safety challenges, faced strict regulatory and political obstacles, had limited economic viability, and saw no strong market demand after their introduction.** In the age of globalization and cost-conscious travel, subsonic aviation simply became more practical and affordable, making supersonic travel commercially unviable.
