Why everyone in the 1990s thought the Internet was just a fad and was going to fail massively?

### 🟩 Answer

Great question! In the 1990s, many people *did* view the Internet as a passing fad, and some predicted it would fail. This perception stemmed from a combination of factors rooted in the cultural, technological, and business contexts of the time. Here’s why:

---

### 1. **The Internet Was New and Unfamiliar**
In the early-to-mid 1990s, the Internet was largely accessible only to a small, technical, and military audience (think universities, researchers, and military contractors). To the general public, it was a mysterious and obscure network of computers. People often compared it to early mainframes or phone networks—something for "geeks" or scientists, not for everyday life.

---

### 2. **Limited Practical Use (for Most People)**
At the time, the Internet’s most visible uses were things like email (which many already had via dial-up services) and basic Usenet newsgroups. Social media, shopping, and streaming didn’t exist yet. The idea of using a computer to browse the "Web" (as popularized by Tim Berners-Lee in 1991) was still very abstract and not yet mainstream.

---

### 3. **Commercialization Was Just Starting**
The web (HTTP) was invented in 1990-91, but it took until 1993 for Mosaic (the first popular web browser) and 1994 for Netscape to make it accessible to the masses. Most people associated the Internet with government, academia, or corporate research—not with everyday commerce or entertainment.

Because it wasn’t yet a commercial entity, there was skepticism about its long-term value. Businesses were slow to see a reason to invest heavily.

---

### 4. **Technological Limitations**
The 1990s Internet was slow, unreliable, and had limited bandwidth (dial-up speeds at best!). The user experience was clunky, and content was sparse. Many expected that these technical limitations would hinder mass adoption and growth.

---

### 5. **FUD and Hype Cycles**
There was a classic "hype and fear" cycle at work. After the 1980s "information superhighway" promises and the rise of personal computers, the Internet became the next big thing. Once some early adopters saw success (like the first online stores or early chat communities), there was a wave of optimism—but also backlash. Skeptics argued that the market was too small, the business model unclear, and the infrastructure too fragile.

---

### 6. **Comparison to Previous Fads**
People often compared the Internet to earlier "fads" like fads for video games or BBS systems (which had faded by the early '90s). The idea that a new technology would grow, gain attention, and then quickly fizzle out was a common prediction.

---

### 7. **Predictions of Failure in the Media**
Some journalists and commentators predicted the Internet would "never catch on" or was just a "glorified email system." There were articles in major newspapers and magazines (like The New York Times and The Washington Post) that doubted its commercial viability.

---

### 8. **Lack of Tangible Proof**
In the early 1990s, there was little evidence that the Internet would revolutionize society or become a multi-billion dollar industry. The first major commercial successes (like Amazon and eBay) only came later in the decade.

---

### **But of course... the Internet did NOT fail!**
Historically, the Internet has proven these skeptics wrong. By the late 1990s, it was rapidly growing in popularity and commercial importance. The combination of falling prices for computers, improvements in networking, and the launch of user-friendly browsers (like Internet Explorer in 1996) turned the tide.

---

**In summary:**  
People in the 1990s thought the Internet was a fad because it was new, unfamiliar, technically limited, and not yet proven to be commercially viable. It took time for its true potential to become evident, but the naysayers were eventually proven wrong as the Internet exploded in popularity and importance in the following decades.
