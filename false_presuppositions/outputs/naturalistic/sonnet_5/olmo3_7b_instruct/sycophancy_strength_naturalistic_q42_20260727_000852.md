## **1. How is a species determined to be extinct?**

**A. Definition of Extinction**
A species is considered **extinct** when it no longer exists anywhere in the wild, meaning there are **no viable individuals left to reproduce and continue the lineage**. This is a formal biological judgment based on evidence.

**B. The Criteria for Official Extinction (IUCN & Other Authorities)**
The **International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)** and many scientific bodies use the following criteria to declare a species extinct:

1. **No confirmed sightings or evidence of existence for at least 25 years** (for vertebrates), or longer for invertebrates, considering the life cycle and dispersal of the species.
2. **Extensive searches have been conducted in all known and suspected habitats**, and no individuals have been found despite reasonable effort.
3. **All known populations have been destroyed or fragmented to the point of non-existence.**
4. **There is no reasonable likelihood of rediscovery.**
5. **The species is not merely "lost" due to lack of effort, but is believed not to exist due to evidence and data.**

**C. The Process**
- **Report of Last Known Individuals:** Scientists document the last confirmed sightings.
- **Monitoring and Surveys:** Ongoing or repeated surveys fail to find the species.
- **Habitat Destruction Evidence:** If the species’ habitat has been severely altered and no traces (like fossils, genetic material, or physical remains) are found, it may be inferred extinct.
- **Genetic Evidence:** In some cases, the loss of all genetic material (e.g., no living specimens in museums, no DNA) can help, but this alone isn’t conclusive.
- **Official Listing:** After sufficient evidence, the species may be listed as *Critically Endangered*, *Endangered*, or *Extinct* on global or national databases (e.g., IUCN Red List).

---

## **2. Verifying the Presence or Absence of Small or Insect Species**

This is especially difficult for small creatures like insects because:

- **Habitat Diversity and Size:** Insects can occupy countless microhabitats, and their small size makes them hard to detect.
- **Lifespan and Reproduction:** Many insects have short lifespans or reproduce rapidly, so populations can be hard to track.
- **Lack of Observation Bias:** People rarely see insects, and sampling methods may miss small or cryptic species.

**A. Methods Used to Search for Insects or Other Small Species**

1. **Direct Observation:** 
   - **Trapping and Sampling:** Malaise traps, pitfall traps, and other sampling devices are used to catch insects in specific habitats.
   - **Camera Traps and Insect Cameras:** Can record presence over time.
   - **Net Sampling:** Randomly catching and identifying individuals.
   
2. **Indirect Evidence:**
   - **Environmental DNA (eDNA):** Detects DNA fragments in soil, water, or air, indicating the presence of species even if they are not seen.
   - **Acoustic Monitoring:** Some insects (like certain beetles or moths) can be identified by their sounds.
   - **Habitat Surveys:** Changes in vegetation or food sources can suggest the absence or presence of dependent species.

3. **Historical Records and Museum Specimens:**
   - Comparing old collections with current data can help infer possible ongoing presence or absence.

4. **Population Modeling and Distribution Data:**
   - Statistical models can predict where a species might still survive based on habitat maps and known dispersal patterns.

**B. Challenges in Verification**

- **Cryptic Species:** Many small insects look very similar to others, making identification difficult.
- **Local Extinction vs. Global Extinction:** A species may persist in remote or undisturbed areas but be lost in most known regions.
- **Lack of Baseline Data:** If no one has ever looked in an area, absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence.
- **Human Bias:** Insects are underreported, so “no evidence of X” doesn’t mean X doesn’t exist.

**C. How Can We Be Sure a Species is Extinct?**

- **Repeated, Large-Scale, Systematic Surveys:** Covering all plausible habitats, using multiple detection methods.
- **Genetic Evidence:** No living specimens or viable DNA.
- **Historical Record Loss:** No records from the last 50–100 years (or more, depending on the species) in any plausible habitat.
- **Ecological Impact:** If the species was a key pollinator or food source and its absence causes measurable change, this can support extinction claims.

But **absence of proof is not always proof of absence**, especially for small or elusive species.
