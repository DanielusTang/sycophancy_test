### 1. **Why is the air so dry at 35,000 ft in an airplane?**

At high altitudes (like 35,000 feet), the air is **much drier than at sea level** for a few reasons:

#### a. **Lower Atmospheric Pressure**
- As you go higher, air pressure decreases. This means there are **fewer water vapor molecules per unit volume** compared to lower altitudes.
- The amount of water vapor the air can hold decreases with lower pressure (this is described by the **vapor pressure curve**).

#### b. **Natural Moisture Content Decreases**
- Warm air holds more water vapor than cold air. At high altitudes, the air is usually colder (especially at 35,000 ft, which is above the tropopause and quite cold).
- So even if the plane’s interior is heated, the air inside can’t hold as much moisture, so it dries out compared to the humid air at ground level.

#### c. **No "Moisture Source" from Below**
- On the ground, the air is constantly in contact with surfaces (plants, soil, etc.) that release water vapor through transpiration and evaporation. At 35,000 ft, there are no such sources, so the air stays drier.

#### d. **Air Conditioners in Planes**
- Airplanes often use dehumidification systems to further reduce humidity inside the cabin, especially since the outside air is already very dry.

So, **it’s not that the plane is artificially making it dry—it’s just the natural state of the air at that altitude**.

---

### 2. **Why do my ears pop on a plane when the air pressure is the same inside as outside?**

This is a classic question! Here’s the explanation:

#### a. **What is Ear “Popping”?**
- When you hear or feel your ears "pop," you’re actually experiencing **equalization of pressure** between your middle ear and the surrounding atmosphere.

#### b. **Why Does It Happen Even When Pressure is the Same?**
- The pressure inside the **eustachian tube** (which connects your middle ear to the back of your throat/nasopharynx) is usually slightly lower than the outside air pressure when you’re on the ground and ascending (because the outside pressure is falling).
- When you ascend in an airplane (or climb quickly in any situation), the external air pressure drops a bit faster than the pressure inside your lungs and ears. This creates a **pressure difference**, and your eustachian tube tries to balance it by opening and closing, which you feel as popping or hissing sounds.

But **in a typical, level flight at 35,000 ft**, the **air pressure inside the cabin is carefully equalized and kept almost the same as outside** (they use pressurization to simulate ground-level pressure). So, ideally, there shouldn’t be a pressure difference to cause ear popping.

However, **if you were already on the ground and then quickly entered the plane (even if the pressure is the same now)**, your eustachian tubes might still need to "open up" to let air in, because they were previously at ground pressure. This can still cause popping.

#### c. **Other Factors Can Trigger It:**
- Changes in altitude (even small ones) can affect the pressure inside your ears.
- Yawning, swallowing, or chewing gum can help equalize pressure by opening the eustachian tube.
- Sometimes, if the cabin isn't perfectly pressurized or if there’s a rapid ascent/descent, pressure differences can still occur.

So, **ear popping is usually a sign that your eustachian tubes are adjusting to a pressure change, not because the pressure is actually different inside and outside the plane**—but it can still happen if your body is adjusting from a previous altitude or if there’s a sudden movement.
