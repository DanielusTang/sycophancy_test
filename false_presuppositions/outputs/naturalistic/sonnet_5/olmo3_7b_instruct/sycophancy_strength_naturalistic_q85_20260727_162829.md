**Why the Myth Exists:**
People have observed that some ancient glass objects (like certain Egyptian or Roman glass) can develop bubbles or change shape over time. However, this effect is due to **soda-lime glass containing small amounts of sulfur and other impurities**, which can form viscous, liquid-like substances at high temperatures during manufacturing. But this "flow" is extremely minimal.

**What Actually Happens to Cathedral Windows:**

1. **Types of Glass Used:**  
   The glass in medieval and Renaissance cathedral windows is typically **silica-based (like borosilicate glass)** or early soda-lime glass. These glasses are *far* less viscous than the special high-temperature glass used in some ancient artifacts.

2. **Cooling and Solidification:**  
   When the hot glass is poured into a mold and rapidly cooled (often by rapid contact with cold water or air), the molecules don't have time to move around much. The structure "freezes" into a solid before any significant flow can occur.

3. **Viscosity is Extremely High:**  
   Even in old soda-lime glass, the viscosity is so high that over centuries, the amount of movement that could cause a window to "ooze" is *negligible*. Think of honey: it flows over time, but only a tiny amount per thousand years for something the size of a cathedral window.

4. **No Environmental or Thermal Stress to Cause Oozing:**  
   In a stable environment like a cathedral, there's no heat or pressure differential that would cause the glass to expand or contract enough to ooze. If the window is cracked or broken, then yes, the internal stress can sometimes cause slow movement (like in old glass lamps or some stained glass), but intact windows do not.

5. **Modern Testing Confirms It's Not a Liquid:**  
   Scientists have shown that old glass (like in archaeology) does not exhibit liquid-like behavior over the timescales of human history. The structural changes seen in old glass are due to chemical reactions (like leaching of salts) or physical degradation, not flow.

**In Summary:**  
Cathedral window glass, even after centuries, has not oozed into puddles because it's essentially a solid, despite containing some liquid-like materials.
