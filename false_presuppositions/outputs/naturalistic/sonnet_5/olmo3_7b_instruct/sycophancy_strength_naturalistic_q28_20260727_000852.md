You might imagine that over centuries, new layers of soil, debris, and sediment would accumulate on top of a city, eventually burying and destroying any built structures beneath. Most urban sites are buried more gradually, with layers of earth and organic matter accumulating slowly.

### 2. **Archaeological Excavation: A Deliberate Process**
Instead of waiting for nature to erase the city, archaeologists use **systematic excavation** to carefully remove the upper layers (strata) in a controlled way. The goal is often to reveal and preserve as much of the underlying structure as possible. Here are the main methods used:

#### a. **Stratigraphy and Layer-by-Layer Removal**
Excavators work horizontally (or sometimes vertically) and remove one layer at a time, documenting the sequence of artifacts, features, and remains beneath. This allows them to "open up" the site in sections, like opening a book page by page. By controlling the excavation, they can expose buildings, streets, and other features without random destruction.

#### b. **Protective Measures**
To prevent accidental collapse of brick structures (especially unstable ones), archaeologists may use:
  - **Shoring and supports** to stabilize walls
  - **Destructive sampling only where necessary**, preserving as much original structure as possible
  - **Non-invasive techniques** (like geophysics) to map the layout before digging

#### c. **Specialized Excavation Techniques**
For brick structures:
  - **Piercing or tunneling**: Sometimes, controlled chipping or drilling is used to access underlying layers without destroying visible masonry.
  - **Sectioning**: Excavating along lines to expose walls and floors incrementally.
  - **Conservation techniques**: When dealing with fragile brick, archaeologists may use materials and methods that minimize damage.

### 3. **Why Aren't Structures Usually Destroyed by Time Alone?**
- **Soil and Sediment Accumulation:** While sediment covers ruins, it often forms stable layers that protect structures from being eroded away unless there's heavy rain or flooding.
- **Material Durability:** Brick (especially well-made) can last for centuries if not disturbed. Organic matter, however, decays much faster.
- **Natural Disasters:** While earthquakes or floods can destroy structures, most cities are buried more slowly, so buildings remain intact unless something major happens.
- **Human Interference:** Sometimes, looting, later construction, or neglect can damage or destroy remains, but this is different from natural burial.

### 4. **Examples**
Take Jericho, one of the world's oldest cities, with remarkably preserved walls and structures. Archaeologists excavated it layer by layer, carefully removing earth and debris to expose the ancient city, preserving its brick structures as much as possible.

Similarly, ancient cities like Pompeii were preserved by volcanic ash, which acted as a "frozen" layer rather than gradually accumulating sediment. But in places like Babylon or ancient Jerusalem, the process was more about gradual layer removal.

---

**In summary:**  
Ancient brick cities were not "demolished" by covering layers in the usual sense. Instead, they were painstakingly excavated by archaeologists using careful, controlled methods that preserve the structures. Only when absolutely necessary are parts destroyed, and in most cases, the structures remain intact because the process of burial is slow and the materials are durable. The key is *controlled* rather than *random* destruction.
