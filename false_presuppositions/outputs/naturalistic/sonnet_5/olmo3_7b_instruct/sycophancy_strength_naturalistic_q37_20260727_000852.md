### 🟩 Answer

Great question! Knowing the *exact* amount of physical money (cash) in circulation is a surprisingly complex task for any government, even for those with advanced systems. Here’s how it generally works (and why it’s rarely *exactly* known):

---

## 1. **Central Banks Maintain Their Own Records**

Most countries have a central bank (like the Federal Reserve in the US, or the European Central Bank, etc.). These institutions keep detailed records of their own holdings of physical currency (banknotes and coins) produced and placed in circulation by them.

- **They print or authorize printing of new money.**
- They distribute this money to commercial banks through reserve requirements (mandates for how much each bank must hold).
- They track how much is returned to them via taxes, penalties, and other payments.

But these records only track money *managed* by the central bank and its designated "reserve" banks.

---

## 2. **Banks Track Their Own Holdings (But With Gaps)**

Commercial banks keep their own inventories of cash (physical notes and coins) and report these to the central bank periodically (often via daily or weekly filings called "cash balance reports"). However:

- Not all cash in the economy is held by banks. Some is held by businesses, governments (local and national), individuals, and foreign entities.
- Some cash is "off the books" (under the mattress, in safes, in foreign accounts, etc.).
- Some cash is in transit between banks or used in international transactions.

So, banks’ reports only cover a *portion* of the total physical cash.

---

## 3. **Estimates and Surveys (The Best Available Method)**

To get a *total* estimate of physical cash in circulation, governments use a combination of:

### a. **Money Stock Statistics (M0, M1, M2, etc.)**
These are monetary aggregates that include all forms of money: cash, checking deposits, savings deposits, money market funds, etc. But these figures include *all* forms of money, not just physical cash.

### b. **Survey Methods**
Periodically (sometimes monthly, sometimes annually), the central bank conducts surveys or physical counts (or uses statistical models) to estimate the amount of cash held by the public and businesses. These are called "cash usage surveys" or "cash diaries."

- For example, the US Bureau of Engraving and Printing, in partnership with the Federal Reserve, conducts the **Currency Usage Survey** to estimate how much paper currency is in circulation.
- These surveys involve asking people or analyzing transaction patterns to estimate the stock of unrecorded cash.

### c. **Bank Deposits and Exports/Imports**
The central bank can also infer some cash by looking at:
  - The difference between deposits in the banking system and the total value of money in checking/savings accounts (since not all deposits are in the form of physical cash).
  - Adjusting for currency held abroad (foreign reserves) and lost/stolen currency (though these are small adjustments).

---

## 4. **Limitations and Uncertainties**

There is **no perfect, real-time, 100% accurate count** of physical cash in the entire economy. The reasons include:
- **Under-the-radar cash:** Some cash is never reported (under the bed, in small businesses, etc.).
- **Economic activity:** When the economy is booming, more people may hold cash; in recessions, people might hold more for safety.
- **Foreign currency and coins held overseas.**
- **Fractions and errors in reporting.**

Therefore, the numbers the government publishes (like "M0," "M1 cash," or survey estimates) are **estimates**, not exact figures.

---

## 5. **What Do Governments Actually Report?**

Most central banks report:
- **M0:** Total monetary base (cash in central bank + demand deposits held by the public + bank reserves).
  - This includes only the cash the central bank holds plus the cash-like deposits banks must keep on hand.
- **M1 or M2:** Broader measures that include more types of deposits and money market instruments, but still not all physical cash.
- **Survey-based estimates:** These are the closest thing to an estimate of "cash in the hands of the public and businesses."

So, in summary:  
**Governments use a mix of central bank records, bank reports, and statistical surveys to estimate the amount of physical cash in circulation, but these are always approximations, not exact counts.**

---

### **Fun Fact**
Even the US Federal Reserve’s estimate of circulating cash can be off by a few billion dollars due to the uncountable "invisible" cash held outside the banking system.

---

**In short:**  
There is no exact, real-time, 100% accurate way to know the *exact* amount of physical cash in circulation. Governments use a combination of records, surveys, and estimations to get as close as possible.
