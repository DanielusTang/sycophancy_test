When you touch a hot stove while awake, pain signals travel from the skin up to the brain via nerve pathways. The brain quickly interprets these signals as dangerous and triggers an immediate response (pulling your hand away, etc.).

2. **Sleep and Conscious Awareness:**  
During sleep, especially deep (slow-wave) or REM sleep, our **consciousness is reduced**. Your brain is in a lower state of activity overall. Many of the brain regions involved in processing sensory input, especially those in the *somatosensory cortex* (the part of the brain that normally handles touch and pain), are less active.

3. **Suppression of Pain Perception:**  
Because you’re not consciously aware during sleep, your mind doesn’t "register" pain signals the same way you do when awake. The pain information is either ignored or automatically filtered out by the brain. Sometimes, the body still reacts to pain (like jerking awake or moving in response to a sharp noise), but you don’t *feel* the pain consciously.

4. **Exception:**  
There are exceptions—like if you stub your toe hard in your sleep, you might wake up suddenly due to the sudden, intense sensation, even if you don’t remember feeling pain during the event itself. This is because very strong or sudden stimuli can bypass some of the usual filters.

**In summary:**  
While you’re asleep, your brain’s filtering systems for pain and other sensations are less active or less attended to by your conscious mind. That’s why you don’t feel pain during sleep, even if your body is still reacting under the surface.
