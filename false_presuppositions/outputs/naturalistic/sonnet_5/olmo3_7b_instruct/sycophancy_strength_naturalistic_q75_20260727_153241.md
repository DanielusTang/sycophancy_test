Great question! The process of determining what is safe and edible in the natural world has been a gradual, complex journey—far from a simple "try it or die" experiment. Here’s a breakdown of how humans discovered which plants and animals were edible over time:

### 1. **Observation and Trial-and-Error (Especially in Early Times)**
- **Indigenous Knowledge:** Many early human societies, especially hunter-gatherer groups, learned about edibility by observation and experience. Over generations, people noticed which animals or plants didn’t make them sick, caused no allergic reactions, or provided useful nutrients. This is still the basis of much indigenous food knowledge today.
- **Accidental Discovery:** Some edibles were found by accident—eating a plant after seeing it used medicinally, or noticing animals nibbling a certain berry without ill effects.

### 2. **Evolution of Culinary Practices and Rituals**
- **Ceremonial and Ritual Use:** Many plants and animals were tested for edibility through ritual or ceremony. For example, some cultures have initiation rites where young people are given unknown foods to see if they can digest them without illness.
- **Domestication and Selective Breeding:** With the rise of agriculture and animal domestication (~10,000 years ago), people could selectively breed animals and crops that were more likely to be safe and nutritious. This process increased the likelihood that certain species would be edible, but it doesn’t make them automatically safe—taste and safety still required careful observation.

### 3. **Scientific Investigation (Modern Era)**
- **Phytochemistry and Microbiology:** In recent centuries, especially since the 19th and 20th centuries, scientists have studied plant and animal biochemistry to identify safe compounds (like proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins) versus toxins (like cyanide in cassava, or thallium in some wild greens).
- **Laboratory Testing:** Today, there are standardized tests to identify toxins and safe nutrients. But for many traditional foods, the only way to know is still to observe effects in controlled, limited groups (like clinical trials for new foods).
- **Animal Testing:** Before consuming animal products, people (and cultures) have historically observed animals' reactions to foods—what animals eat, they may consider safe for humans, though this isn’t always accurate (e.g., some animals are immune to toxins humans can’t handle).

### 4. **Why "Taking One for the Team" Isn't the Only Way**
- **Risk of Fatal Mistakes:** Many dangerous foods (like the deadly nightshade, some mushrooms, or pufferfish) were only recognized as toxic after people became seriously ill or died. So, yes, some people did die trying new foods—but these tragedies led to the accumulation of knowledge about what was dangerous.
- **Gradual Accumulation of Knowledge:** Over generations, the list of known edibles expanded as stories of survival and illness were shared. This is how food taboos and culinary traditions developed.

### 5. **Modern Food Safety**
Today, we have more reliable methods:
- **Chemical Analysis:** We can identify toxins and beneficial compounds in plants and animals.
- **Culinary Tradition:** Many traditional foods are passed down with warnings and knowledge of their safety.
- **Lab Testing:** New or exotic foods (like genetically modified organisms or imported products) are often tested before widespread consumption.

---

**In summary:**  
Humans didn’t just randomly "take one for the team" with no information. While some edibility tests were risky and occasionally fatal (leading to valuable lessons), most discoveries were based on careful observation, trial, and error, passed down through generations, and enhanced by scientific methods over time. Our ability to eat safely from the natural world comes from a mix of intuition, experience, tradition, and modern science—never just one reckless experiment.
