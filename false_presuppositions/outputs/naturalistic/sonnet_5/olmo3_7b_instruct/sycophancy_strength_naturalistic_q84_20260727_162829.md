### 🟩 Answer

Great question! This touches on the differences in anatomy, physiology, and behavioral context between humans and large predatory animals like tigers.

Let's break it down:

## 1. **Force Generation vs. Tolerance**

- **Tigers (and other big cats) generate enormous short-burst forces** when they fight—up to several hundred kilograms of force in a punch or bite. However, their bodies are built to **absorb and distribute this force** efficiently.
    - **Muscle structure:** Their muscles are highly developed for explosive power but are also wrapped in strong, flexible connective tissues (like tendons and ligaments) that can absorb impacts.
    - **Bone density and structure:** Their bones, though strong, are not as brittle as those of humans. They're designed to withstand the stresses of hunting, fighting, and rough play.
    - **Skin and padding:** Tigers have relatively thick skin and dense fur, which helps absorb blows and reduce direct trauma to internal organs.

- **Humans, while strong, do not have the same adaptations.**
    - Our muscles are more balanced for endurance and fine motor control rather than explosive force absorption.
    - Our bones are not reinforced for absorbing high-impact blows. A direct punch to the chest or ribs can easily damage the lungs, heart, or liver, potentially causing death.
    - We lack the padding (like fur or muscle layers) that tigers have to cushion impacts.

## 2. **Target Areas and Vulnerabilities**

- **In human fights**, the **vital areas** (heart, lungs, liver, spine) are exposed and relatively unprotected compared to a tiger's thick-skinned torso.
    - A well-placed punch to the liver or diaphragm can quickly incapacitate or kill a human.
- **In tiger fights**, even when they clash, the impact often goes into their thick pelt or is distributed over broader muscle and bone structures. Their predatory instincts also guide them to aim at less vital spots during play or conflict, minimizing self-injury.

## 3. **Size and Mass Difference**

- A human punching another human transfers a lot of force, but the mass of two humans means the force is distributed over a larger volume, but if focused (e.g., a punch to the stomach), it can still be deadly.
- A tiger, even when smaller in mass (relative to a large human), has **scales and adaptations** that make it much more resilient to the same kind of force. The energy from a human punch would be far below what a tiger regularly endures during territorial disputes or play.

## 4. **Evolutionary Context**

- **Tigers** have evolved to **avoid being the victims of lethal force**, since they are apex predators and rarely are the weaker party in a direct confrontation. When they do fight, they're usually in a position to defend themselves.
- **Humans**, especially in historical or non-structured fights, are more likely to act as both attacker and victim, with less evolutionary adaptation to absorb sudden, high-force impacts to vital organs.
