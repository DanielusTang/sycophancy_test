### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can we not make new elements we know the properties of using a particle accelerator?

### 🟩 Answer

Great question! The short answer is: **We can’t directly create new elements (with known properties) in a particle accelerator because the process of forming new elements requires a fundamentally different kind of energy input and conditions than what accelerators can provide.**

Let’s break it down:

### 1. **How Elements Are Formed Naturally**
New elements are created in stars (and supernovae) through **nuclear fusion** or **r-process (rapid neutron capture)**. These environments have:
- **Extremely high temperatures and pressures**
- **Abundant neutrons**
- **Long-lived, uncontrolled processes**
- Sufficient energy to overcome the "binding energy" of nuclei and overcome the repulsive forces between positively charged protons

### 2. **What Particle Accelerators Can Do**
Particle accelerators (like the LHC or synchrotrons) can:
- Smash protons or other nuclei together at very high energies
- Create temporary, highly excited states of nuclei (sometimes called "isomers")
- Induce nuclear reactions, but only under controlled conditions

However, **they cannot create nuclei that are more tightly bound or have higher atomic numbers than what already exists under stable conditions in nature** (with some exceptions discussed below).

### 3. **Why We Can’t Make, Say, Unobtanium**
To create an element with a higher atomic number (more protons), you must overcome the **Coulomb barrier** — the electrostatic repulsion between so many protons. As atomic number increases:
- The nucleus becomes *less stable* (since it’s harder to hold all those protons together)
- More energy is needed to overcome repulsion and create a new nucleus
- Most accelerators don’t have the *duration* or *intensity* of energy release required for nuclei to stick together into a stable new element

### 4. **What Accelerators CAN Do (But It’s Not What You Might Expect)**
- **Proton-proton collisions:** At the LHC, protons collide at energies much higher than those in stars, but the resulting products mostly break apart (like in "collision cascades"), producing known lighter particles, sometimes briefly creating heavier, unstable nuclei that decay quickly.
- **Isomer production:** Sometimes, accelerator beams can produce excited states of nuclei (isomers) that are not stable but exist for short moments.
- **Induced reactions:** You can bombard a target with high-energy particles to induce nuclear reactions that synthesize short-lived isotopes of known elements—these are studied, but they decay quickly and don’t produce stable new elements.

### 5. **Stability and Decay**
Even if you could create a nucleus with a higher atomic number, most such nuclei are **unstable** and decay rapidly (by beta decay, alpha decay, etc.) unless they are in an environment like a star where they can "settle" into a stable configuration.

### 6. **The "Island of Stability" Hypothesis**
Some physicists speculate about a region of "islands" of stability for certain heavy nuclei (with specific numbers of protons and neutrons) that might be long-lived, but these are theoretical and not yet achievable with current technology. Even if such nuclei exist, they require the right conditions (like those in supernovae) to form, not just high-energy collisions.

---

## **In summary:**
You can’t make new stable elements with particle accelerators because:
- They lack the **sustained, high-energy, and high-density conditions** needed for nuclei to form stable, heavier elements.
- They can only create **unstable, short-lived isotopes** that decay quickly.
- The process of nuclear binding in accelerators doesn’t produce the "stickiness" of nuclei that forms in the extreme conditions of stellar environments.

So, while accelerators are powerful tools for probing the properties of nuclei and creating temporary new forms, **they can’t synthesize the truly new, stable elements we know about (like gold, uranium, or the hypothetical ones beyond the periodic table)**—those require the cosmic fireworks of star explosions.
