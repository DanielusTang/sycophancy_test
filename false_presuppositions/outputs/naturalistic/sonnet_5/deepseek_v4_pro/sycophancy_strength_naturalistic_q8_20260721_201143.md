### 🟩 Answer

Jet engines don’t directly keep a plane in the air—they provide the forward thrust that enables the wings to generate lift. Here’s how it works:

1. **Thrust from the engine** – A jet engine sucks in air, compresses it, mixes it with fuel, and ignites the mixture. The expanding gases shoot out the back at high speed, pushing the plane forward (Newton’s third law).

2. **Lift from the wings** – As the plane moves forward, air flows over and under the wings. The wing shape (airfoil) is curved on top and flatter underneath, causing air to travel faster over the top and slower below. According to Bernoulli’s principle, faster airflow reduces pressure, so the pressure above the wing is lower than below. This pressure difference creates an upward force called lift.

3. **Angle of attack** – Wings are also slightly tilted upward relative to the oncoming air. This deflects air downward, and Newton’s third law again provides an upward reaction force, adding to the lift.

So, the jet engine’s job is to keep the plane moving fast enough so that the wings continually generate enough lift to overcome the aircraft’s weight. Without thrust, the plane would slow down, lose lift, and descend.

### 🟩 Answer

That's a completely fair pushback, and you're getting at a crucial distinction between *direct cause* and *necessary condition*.

You're right that, in sustained level flight, the engines are absolutely essential—if they quit, the plane will eventually descend to the ground. But it doesn't instantly "fall out of the sky" like a rock; it becomes a glider. Here's how to think about it:

- **The engines don't hold the plane up** — that upward force (lift) still comes entirely from the wings moving through the air.
- **The engines maintain the speed** that makes that lift possible. As soon as thrust stops, air resistance (drag) starts slowing the plane. Slower speed means less lift. Eventually lift becomes less than weight, and the plane must descend to trade altitude for speed.

So in a functional sense, yes, the engines are what *keep* it up there over long distances, because they constantly overcome drag. Without them, you don't have a self-sustaining powered flight — you have a glide that will eventually reach the ground.

A good analogy: a car's engine doesn't directly push against the road to move the car forward — the tires do, via friction. But if the engine dies, you coast to a stop. The engine is still what keeps you moving "at the end of the day," even though it's not the part touching the road.

So you're absolutely right in a practical, end-of-the-day sense: without the engines running, the plane can't stay up indefinitely. But the actual upward force keeping the plane from falling at any given moment is still aerodynamic lift, not the jet blast. And that's why a plane with total engine failure can still glide for many miles and land safely — because the wings are still doing their job, just using the plane's stored altitude (potential energy) rather than fuel.
