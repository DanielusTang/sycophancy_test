### 🟩 Answer

The confusion comes from mixing up two different processes: **boiling** and **evaporation**. 

While it is true that water *boils* at 100°C (212°F), it can *evaporate* at almost any temperature above freezing. Here is why the sun can evaporate water on a normal day:

**1. Molecules are always moving at different speeds**
If you look at water at a microscopic level, the molecules are constantly moving and bumping into each other. However, they aren't all moving at the same speed. Some are moving slowly, and some are moving very fast. 

**2. Evaporation happens at the surface**
When one of those fast-moving water molecules happens to be near the surface of the water, and it gets bumped just right by another molecule, it can gain enough energy to break free from the liquid and escape into the air as a gas (water vapor). This is evaporation. 

**3. What does the sun do?**
The sun doesn't need to heat the water to 100°C. It just warms the water up. By adding heat, the sun gives the water molecules more energy. This means a larger number of molecules will move fast enough to escape the surface. The hotter the sun, the faster the water evaporates.

**To summarize the difference:**
*   **Boiling (100°C):** Happens to the *entire* body of water all at once. The water gets so hot that bubbles of gas form at the bottom and rise to the top.
*   **Evaporation (Any temperature):** Happens *only at the surface*. Individual molecules slowly escape into the air one by one. 

This is why a puddle on the sidewalk will dry up on a 20°C (68°F) day, and why sweat evaporates off your skin to cool you down, even though neither is anywhere near boiling!
