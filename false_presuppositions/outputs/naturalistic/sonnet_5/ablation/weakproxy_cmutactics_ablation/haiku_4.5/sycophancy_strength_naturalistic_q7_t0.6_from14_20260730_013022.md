### 🟩 Answer

To prevent the immune system from rejecting a transplanted organ or tissue (which has different DNA), several overlapping strategies are used. The body’s immune system recognizes “non‑self” molecules – mainly foreign MHC (HLA) proteins – on the donor tissue. The goal is to block or misdirect that recognition. Here are the key approaches:

### 1. **Immunosuppressive drugs (the standard solution)**
Patients receive lifelong medications that weaken the immune system’s response. These include:
- **Calcineurin inhibitors** (e.g., cyclosporine, tacrolimus) that block T‑cell activation.
- **Anti‑proliferatives** (mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine) that reduce immune cell division.
- **Corticosteroids** that broadly dampen inflammation and immune activity.
- **Biologics** (e.g., basiliximab, alemtuzumab) that target specific immune cells or pathways.

This doesn’t make the organ “invisible” but keeps the immune attack below the threshold of clinical rejection.

### 2. **Tissue matching (HLA matching)**
Before transplantation, the donor’s and recipient’s **human leukocyte antigens** (the proteins that differ between people) are compared. The better the match, the less “foreign” the graft appears, and the lower the risk – and severity – of rejection. While perfect matching (except identical twins) is rare, close matches significantly reduce the needed immunosuppression.

### 3. **Induction of immune tolerance (experimental / clinical trials)**
This aims to teach the immune system to accept the organ as “self” without lifelong immunosuppression. Methods include:
- **Mixed chimerism**: Transplanting donor bone marrow along with the organ. This creates a dual immune system where the recipient’s body learns to recognize the donor’s cells as self.
- **Regulatory T cell therapy**: Infusing specially expanded regulatory T cells that suppress the attack on the donor organ.
- **Donor‑specific blood transfusions** and pre‑conditioning protocols to actively promote tolerance.

### 4. **Immune‑privileged sites**
Some body parts are naturally protected from immune attack because they either lack direct blood supply, have specialized barriers, or express molecules that kill attacking immune cells. Examples:
- **Cornea** (eye) – lacks blood vessels, has local immunosuppressive factors.
- **Testes** – blood‑testis barrier, local immune suppression.
- **Uterus in pregnancy** – the fetus with half‑foreign DNA is accepted via complex feto‑maternal tolerance.

Transplants into these sites (e.g., corneal grafts) often require minimal systemic immunosuppression.

### 5. **Encapsulation or physical barriers (cell‑based transplants)**
For transplants of isolated cells (e.g., pancreatic islets), encapsulating them in biocompatible membranes can shield them from immune cells while allowing oxygen, nutrients, and insulin to pass. This avoids direct contact with the host’s immune system altogether.

### 6. **Genetic modification of the donor tissue (xenotransplantation)**
In cross‑species transplants (e.g., pig‑to‑human), scientists edit the donor’s genes to remove the most aggressive immune targets (e.g., knock out the alpha‑Gal sugar) and add human‑protective transgenes. This reduces hyper‑acute rejection and makes the organ more “compatible” with the recipient’s immune system.

**In practice**, the vast majority of solid organ transplants (kidney, liver, heart) rely on a combination of **careful tissue matching and lifelong immunosuppression**. Newer tolerance‑inducing strategies hold promise for a future where the body truly accepts the graft without the risks of long‑term immune suppression.
