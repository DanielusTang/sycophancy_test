### 🟩 Answer

Rainbows are formed when light is refracted, reflected, and dispersed by water droplets—but the light source needs to meet very specific conditions for a classic arc to appear. While it’s true that any light can in principle be split into colors by a droplet, practical rainbows almost exclusively come from the sun. Here’s why:

### 1. The light must act like a distant point source
Sunlight reaches Earth as nearly parallel rays because the sun is so far away (93 million miles). This means every droplet in a rain shower sees light coming from exactly the same direction. The rainbow appears at a fixed angle (about 42° for the primary bow) relative to the line from the sun to your eye, and all droplets at that angle contribute their colors to the same arc.

Street lamps and building lights are close, extended sources. Their light rays spread out in many directions, so each droplet receives light from different parts of the lamp at different angles. The result is a messy overlap of countless tiny rainbow-like reflections that blur together into a washed‑out glow, not a sharp, distinct bow.

### 2. You need a large, uniformly illuminated curtain of water
A rainbow requires millions of droplets spread across a wide area (like a rainstorm or a misty waterfall), all lit from behind you. The sun, being immense and bright, lights up an entire rain shaft evenly. Artificial lights are too weak and directional to illuminate a broad enough field of droplets. Even a powerful streetlight only lights up a small patch of rain directly beneath it, and you’d be looking down into the light rather than having it behind you at the correct geometry.

### 3. The observer’s geometry is critical
For a rainbow, the light source, the water droplets, and your eyes must form a cone with the anti‑solar point at your head’s shadow. With the sun low in the sky (morning/afternoon), the 42° cone extends upward and creates a tall arc. Streetlights are typically mounted high and near you, so the “anti‑lamp” point would be on the ground far away—not a recipe for a visible arc. If you try to position yourself between a lamp and a rain shower, the lamp’s brightness often overwhelms the faint return from the droplets.

### 4. Brightness and continuous spectrum matter
Rainbows stand out because sunlight is incredibly intense and contains a continuous spectrum, producing vivid, smooth color bands. Most artificial lights (LED, sodium vapor, fluorescent) have spiky or limited spectra. A sodium streetlight, for instance, might produce only a monochromatic yellow smear, not a rainbow. Even white LEDs lack the brightness to make the phenomenon visible against ambient light.

**Can you ever see a “rainbow” from an artificial light?**  
Yes, but it won’t be a grand arc across the sky. You might spot a tiny color fringe in the spray of a fountain directly illuminated by a very bright, point‑like spotlight (like a theatrical follow spot) if you stand in just the right place. Similarly, a full moon—which is a distant, reflected sunlight source—can create a faint *moonbow*. But everyday streetlights or building lights simply don’t have the right combination of distance, brightness, and parallel rays to paint nature’s spectacular bow.
