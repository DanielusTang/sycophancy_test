### 🟩 Answer

The difference comes down to how electrons are arranged inside the materials. Let’s break it down.

### Why some plastics are transparent and others are opaque

Plastics are made of long polymer chains. At the microscopic level, these chains can arrange in two main ways:

- **Amorphous (disordered):** The chains are tangled randomly, like a bowl of spaghetti. Light passes through without much scattering, so the material looks **transparent** (think acrylic, polycarbonate, or clear glass-like cups).
- **Semi‑crystalline:** Parts of the chains fold into tiny, ordered crystals while the rest stays amorphous. The boundaries between these crystals and the disordered regions scatter light in all directions. That scattering makes the plastic look **translucent or opaque** (like a milk jug or a white polypropylene container).

Additives (colorants, fillers, even air bubbles) can also make a plastic opaque. So by controlling the crystallinity and additives, you get either transparent or opaque plastics.

### Why metals are never transparent (in bulk)

Metals have a completely different structure. Instead of localized bonds, metal atoms share a “sea” of free electrons that can move throughout the whole solid. These electrons are the key:

1. **No band gap** – In insulators and transparent materials, electrons need a large energy jump to absorb visible light. In metals, the conduction band and valence band overlap; there is no gap. Visible light photons have just the right energy to be instantaneously taken up by those free electrons.

2. **Light is absorbed and re‑emitted** – When a light wave hits a metal, the free electrons oscillate with the wave. This absorbs the photon’s energy, then almost instantly re‑radiates it as a reflected wave. That’s why metals are shiny. Any light that isn’t reflected gets absorbed very quickly (within nanometers) and turned into heat.

3. **No transmission** – Because absorption is so strong, a light wave can’t travel through. Even a very thin metal film (like gold leaf) looks semi‑transparent but still heavily tinted and absorbing. A bulk piece of metal blocks all visible light.

### Can we ever make a “transparent metal”?

We do have materials that are **transparent and electrically conductive**, like indium tin oxide (ITO) used in smartphone screens. But these are not true metals—they are wide‑bandgap semiconductors doped to have some free carriers, while still keeping a band gap large enough that visible light isn’t absorbed.

For a material to be a true metal (with a sea of free electrons) **and** transparent in bulk, you’d have to eliminate that strong interaction with light. That would mean removing the free electrons—but then it wouldn’t be a metal anymore. So a thick slab of ordinary metallic iron, aluminium, or copper will always be opaque.

In short:
- **Plastics** can be transparent or opaque because we can control their internal structure (crystallinity) and scattering centers.
- **Metals** are inherently opaque because their free electrons absorb and reflect visible light almost perfectly. No free electrons, no metal—so no bulk transparent metal.
