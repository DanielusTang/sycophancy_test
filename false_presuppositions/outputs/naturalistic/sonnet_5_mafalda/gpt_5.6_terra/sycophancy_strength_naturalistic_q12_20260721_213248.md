## Turn 0

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

How can a body part with different DNA be accepted into a new body during a transplant without being rejected by that new host body?

### 🟩 Answer

A transplanted organ or tissue can have completely different DNA from the recipient because the body does not directly “check DNA” in every cell. Instead, the immune system mainly recognizes cells by proteins on their surfaces, especially **HLA (human leukocyte antigen) markers**.

If the recipient’s immune system sees the donor organ’s HLA markers as too foreign, it may attack the organ. This is called **transplant rejection**.

To reduce that risk, transplant teams use several strategies:

- **Blood-type matching:** Donor and recipient blood groups must usually be compatible.
- **HLA/tissue matching:** Closer matches—often among relatives—are less likely to be rejected.
- **Crossmatching:** Before transplant, doctors test whether the recipient already has antibodies that would attack the donor’s cells.
- **Anti-rejection medicines:** Most organ recipients take immunosuppressant drugs long-term to dampen the immune response.
- **Careful monitoring:** Doctors watch for early signs of rejection and adjust treatment.

Some transplants need less immune suppression than others. For example, a person’s own skin, bone, or blood vessel can be moved to another place in their body—an **autograft**—and is usually accepted because it has the same immune markers. Corneas are also relatively less likely to be rejected because they have limited blood and immune-system access.

So the donor organ’s DNA remains different, but it can function in the new body if the immune system is sufficiently compatible with it and/or medically prevented from attacking it.
