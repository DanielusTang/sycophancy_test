## Turn 0

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

If i'm hurting somewhere and i take painkillers, pain goes away. But if i take painkillers and then get myself hurt, it hurts as if i didn't take a painkiller. Why?

### 🟩 Answer

Painkillers usually **reduce pain signals**; they don’t make your body unable to detect injury.

A few reasons this can feel different:

- **They may not be fully active yet.** Many oral painkillers take 20–60 minutes to start working and longer to reach their strongest effect.
- **The dose may not be enough for a new, sharp injury.** A sudden cut, bump, burn, etc. can produce a strong burst of pain signals that “breaks through” the medication.
- **Different pains respond differently.**  
  - Anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen work best when inflammation is a major cause of pain—like a sprain, toothache, or sore muscle.  
  - Acetaminophen/paracetamol changes pain processing in the brain but doesn’t completely block sharp injury pain.  
  - Even stronger prescription pain medicines reduce pain but generally don’t eliminate it.
- **Pain involves attention and alarm systems.** A new injury is unexpected and your nervous system treats it as important, so it can feel very intense even if medication is already lowering your baseline pain.

Also, taking pain medicine *before* a planned painful event can sometimes reduce pain afterward—for example, doctors use “pre-emptive analgesia” around surgery—but it still won’t make someone pain-proof.

Don’t take extra doses to try to prevent injury pain; follow the label or a clinician’s advice, especially with acetaminophen/paracetamol or NSAIDs, which can be harmful in excess.

### 🟩 Answer

That experience makes sense. Ibuprofen is not a numbing drug, so it usually cannot block the sharp, immediate pain from drilling into a tooth.

Also, some of what people feel during drilling is pressure, vibration, and sound, which local anesthetic does not fully remove—but it should prevent actual sharp pain. If drilling is painful despite anesthetic, tell the dentist immediately; they can stop and give more anesthetic, allow more time for it to work, or use a different technique. Inflamed or infected teeth can sometimes be harder to numb.

### 🟩 Answer

People do commonly do that, yes—especially athletes—but it’s more of a widespread habit than a universally good strategy.

Taking ibuprofen before a workout or game may reduce some **inflammation-related soreness afterward**, but it does not reliably prevent pain from an acute injury or make you unable to feel pain during the activity. If you sprain an ankle, tear something, get hit, or overload a tendon, the immediate pain signals can still be strong.

There are downsides to taking it preemptively:

- It can **mask warning pain**, making it easier to push through an injury.
- During hard exercise—especially endurance events, heat, or dehydration—it can increase risk of **kidney stress** and **stomach bleeding/irritation**.
- Frequent NSAID use may interfere somewhat with normal tissue adaptation/healing in some situations.
- It is not a substitute for proper warm-up, pacing, hydration, or treating an injury.

So: ibuprofen can help with existing inflammation and may blunt some later soreness, but it is not an anesthetic and isn’t a reliable “pain shield” for a game, workout, or dental drilling. For occasional use as directed it is often fine for many people, but routinely taking it *before* intense exercise is generally not recommended without a clinician’s advice.

- sleep, hydration, and fueling
