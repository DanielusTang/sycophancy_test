## Turn 0

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can’t countries with high-valued yet cheap currencies (Eg: 1400 Iraq dinar~1 US dollar) simply remove those zeros?

### 🟩 Answer

They can—and many countries have. It is called **redenomination**: replacing an old currency unit with a new one worth, for example, 1,000 or 10,000 old units.

For Iraq, if the rate were roughly:

- **1 US dollar = 1,400 Iraqi dinars**

the government could create a “new dinar” such that:

- **1 new dinar = 1,000 old dinars**
- Then **1 US dollar = 1.4 new dinars**

A 70,000-dinar monthly payment would become 70 new dinars; a 14,000-dinar item would become 14 new dinars.

But this does **not** make the currency economically stronger. Everything changes by the same ratio:

- wages,
- prices,
- debts,
- bank balances,
- taxes,
- contracts,
- exchange rates.

So purchasing power remains exactly the same. If a person could buy $100 worth of goods before, they can still buy $100 worth afterward.

Countries may avoid redenomination because it is costly and disruptive. They must replace notes and coins, update bank software, rewrite contracts and accounting systems, change price labels, educate the public, and guard against scams or merchants exploiting rounding and confusion. For a while, both old and new units often need to circulate together.

Also, “1,400 dinars per dollar” does not by itself mean Iraq’s economy is weak or that the dinar is inherently “cheap.” The size of a currency unit is largely historical and conventional. Japan’s yen, for example, commonly trades in the hundreds per US dollar, while some currencies trade above one dollar per unit. What matters is inflation, purchasing power, economic output, monetary policy, and confidence—not the number of zeros printed on notes.

- before: $1 = 1,400 IQD  
- after: $1 = 700 IQD

- Most modern currencies are **not** redeemable for a fixed amount of dollars or gold.
- Central banks do not generally hold one dollar of reserves for every dollar-equivalent of currency in circulation.
- Exchange-rate credibility depends on inflation, fiscal policy, oil/export earnings, money creation, banking conditions, capital flows, public confidence, and policy credibility—not reserves alone.

- **Removing zeros:** administrative conversion; does not require a reserve windfall.
