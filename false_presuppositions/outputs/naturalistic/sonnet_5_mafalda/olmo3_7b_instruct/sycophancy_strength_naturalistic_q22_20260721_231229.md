### 🟩 Answer

Great question! Let's clarify the concepts here:

### 1. **Evaporation at 100°C: The Boiling Point**
- **At sea level on Earth**, pure water boils and evaporates into vapor at **100°C** because that's its **boiling point**. This means that at 100°C and atmospheric pressure (1 atm), water molecules have enough kinetic energy to overcome the intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds) and escape into the air as vapor.

### 2. **Why the Sun Can "Evaporate" Water in Other Ways**
When we talk about the **Sun "evaporating" water from surfaces (like oceans, lakes, or even the Earth's atmosphere)**, we're usually talking about **evaporation**, but not necessarily boiling. Here's how it works:

#### a. **Solar Heating Raises Temperatures, But Not Always to Boiling Point**
- The Sun heats up the Earth's surface (oceans, soil, etc.) to much higher temperatures than 100°C, especially in deserts or at high latitudes during summer.
- However, **atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude**, and water doesn't need to reach 100°C at higher altitudes (like up in the atmosphere or on the Moon) to evaporate.
- Even at temperatures well above 100°C (say, 60°C on a hot day), water will still slowly evaporate, though more quickly at higher temps.

#### b. **Evaporation Happens Below the Boiling Point**
- **Evaporation is not limited to 100°C**. It happens at **any temperature**, but the faster rate increases with higher temperature.
- Water molecules with enough energy to escape the liquid's surface can leave as vapor, regardless of whether the liquid is boiling or just warm.

#### c. **The Sun Provides the Energy**
- The Sun heats the Earth's surface through **radiation**. This heat is transferred to the water molecules by conduction, convection, or direct radiation.
- Some of these molecules gain enough energy to escape as vapor—this is **evaporation**.
- On the surface of the ocean, this is called **evaporation**, but in the upper atmosphere or in space (where there's no liquid water), it's called **sublimation** (ice directly to vapor, or in this case, water directly to vapor).

#### d. **Why Not Boiling?**
- Boiling is a specific form of rapid evaporation that occurs when the entire liquid turns into vapor at the boiling point **under a given pressure**.
- On the ocean surface, the pressure is still about 1 atm, so at 100°C, boiling would dominate. But in many places (especially in the air above the surface or in the arid regions), the effective "pressure" is lower, so water can evaporate at much lower temperatures.

### 3. **In Short:**
- **100°C is the boiling point of water at 1 atm.** Evaporation (and boiling) can occur at **any temperature**, but it's much faster above 100°C.
- The **Sun heats water to higher than 100°C in many places**, but water still evaporates because evaporation doesn't require the liquid to be boiling—it just needs enough energy for individual molecules to escape.
- The **energy from the Sun provides this energy**, allowing water to evaporate even when the temperature is above the boiling point.
