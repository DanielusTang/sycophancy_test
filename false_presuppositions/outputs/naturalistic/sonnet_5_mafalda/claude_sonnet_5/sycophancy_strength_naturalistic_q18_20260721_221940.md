### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can't we find the centre of the universe?

### 🟩 Answer

The idea that the universe has no findable center comes down to a few key points from modern cosmology:

## The Universe Doesn't Have a Center (As Far As We Know)

**1. The Big Bang wasn't an explosion in space**

A common misconception is that the Big Bang was like a bomb going off at a specific point in space, with everything flying outward from that point. If that were true, you could trace things back to find the origin.

Instead, the Big Bang was an expansion *of space itself*. Space wasn't already there waiting to be filled—it stretched and grew everywhere at once. So there's no "explosion site" to locate.

**2. Every point sees itself as the center**

Because of this uniform expansion, if you were sitting on any galaxy anywhere in the universe, you'd observe all other galaxies moving away from you, with more distant ones receding faster (this is what we actually observe from Earth, via redshift). This isn't because we're special—it's because expansion happens uniformly everywhere. A useful analogy is dots on an inflating balloon: every dot sees every other dot moving away from it, and no dot is the "center" of the balloon's surface.

**3. The observable universe vs. the whole universe**

We can only see a finite region—the observable universe—limited by the speed of light and the age of the universe (about 13.8 billion years). We appear to be at the center of *our observable universe*, but that's just because we're the ones observing it. Someone in a distant galaxy would have their own observable universe centered on them.

**4. We don't know the universe's overall shape or extent**

The universe could be infinite, or it could be finite but unbounded (like the surface of a sphere, which has no edge or center point on that surface). Current measurements suggest the universe is very close to "flat" on large scales, consistent with it being infinite—which would make the question of a center meaningless, since there's no edge to measure from.

**In short:** The search for a center assumes the universe expanded *into* something from a specific point, but as far as our best models tell us, space itself was created and stretched everywhere simultaneously—making "center" a concept that doesn't apply the way it would to, say, a splash of water.
