### 🟩 Answer

To understand how a transplanted organ is accepted, it helps to first understand why the body wants to reject it. 

The immune system doesn’t actually "see" the foreign DNA inside the cells. Instead, it reads the **proteins on the outside of the cells**, which are built by that DNA. These proteins act like biological ID badges. In humans, the most important of these are called **Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLA)**. 

When the immune system patrols the body and finds cells with the wrong "ID badges" (foreign HLA), it assumes they are dangerous invaders—like a virus or bacteria—and attacks them. 

To prevent the new body from destroying the transplanted organ, doctors rely on two main strategies, along with a few biological exceptions.

### 1. Tissue Matching (Finding similar "ID Badges")
Before a transplant, doctors perform a process called "tissue typing." They look for a donor whose blood type and HLA markers closely match the recipient's. 
* Because DNA is inherited, siblings or parents are often the best matches. 
* Identical twins have the exact same DNA, meaning their HLA markers are identical. Transplants between identical twins are the only ones where rejection is not a risk.
* For everyone else, doctors look for a "close enough" match. The more similar the donor's HLA markers are to the recipient's, the less aggressively the immune system will attack the new organ.

### 2. Immunosuppressant Medications (Suppressing the attack)
Because a perfect match is incredibly rare, the host's immune system *will* eventually realize the DNA is different and try to attack the organ. To stop this, the recipient must take **immunosuppressant (anti-rejection) drugs** for the rest of their life.
* These medications work by chemically dampening the immune system—specifically targeting the white blood cells (T-cells) that would normally coordinate the attack on the new organ.
* By keeping the immune system sedated, the organ is allowed to survive and function.
* **The trade-off:** Because the immune system is suppressed, transplant recipients are at a much higher risk for everyday infections (like colds and the flu) and certain types of cancer, because their bodies cannot fight them off as easily.

### 3. "Immune-Privileged" Body Parts
There is a fascinating exception to the rule of rejection. Certain parts of the body are completely isolated from the immune system. 
The most common example is the **cornea** (the clear front layer of the eye). The cornea has no blood vessels, meaning the white blood cells of the immune system cannot travel to it. Therefore, a doctor can transplant a cornea from one person to another without doing any tissue matching and without the need for lifelong immunosuppressant drugs.

### The Future: Immune Tolerance
Currently, scientists are working on ways to trick the host body into permanently accepting foreign DNA without the need for lifelong drugs. One experimental method involves transplanting bone marrow (stem cells) from the organ donor into the recipient at the same time as the organ. The goal is to create a "chimera" immune system—one that recognizes both the host's original body and the new organ as "self."
