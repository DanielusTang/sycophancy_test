### 🟩 Answer

The short answer is: **No, the rights were usually not secured forever, nor were they secured for every type of technology.**

Music licensing is one of the most complex and expensive parts of the entertainment industry. When you notice that a song has been changed in a movie or TV show—especially when moving from its original release to DVD or streaming—it almost always comes down to the fine print of the original contract. 

Here is a breakdown of why this happens:

### 1. The "New Technology" Problem (Format Rights)
When a studio licenses a song, they don't just buy the right to use it however they want. They buy a license for **specific formats**. 
For example, if a movie was made in 1995, the studio likely paid for the rights to play the song in movie theaters, on television broadcasts, and on VHS tape. Because DVDs, Blu-rays, and streaming services did not exist yet, they were not included in the contract. When the studio wants to put that 1995 movie on Netflix, they have to go back to the musicians and negotiate a brand new contract for "streaming rights." 

### 2. Time Limits
To save money, film and TV producers often license music for a limited period—such as 5, 10, or 15 years—rather than "in perpetuity" (forever). They assume that by the time the license expires, the movie will have made its money and won't be as popular. When that time limit runs out, the studio must either pay to renew the license or remove the song. 

### 3. The Cost of Success
If an indie movie or a new TV show uses a song by a relatively unknown band, the initial license might be very cheap. But if that movie becomes a massive cult classic, the band (or their record label) knows the studio desperately wants to keep the song in the film. When it comes time to renegotiate for streaming or Blu-ray rights, the record label might demand a massive amount of money. If the studio doesn't want to pay it, they will simply swap the song out for a cheaper, generic track.

### 4. Split Rights (The Legal Nightmare)
To use a piece of music in a movie, a studio actually has to get **two** different licenses:
*   **The Sync License:** Pays the person who *wrote* the song (the publisher/songwriter).
*   **The Master License:** Pays the person who *performed* the specific recording (the record label/artist).

If a studio is trying to renew the rights for a streaming release, they have to get both parties to agree. If the songwriter says yes, but the record label says no (or demands too much money), the studio cannot use the song. 

### Famous Examples:
While this happens in movies, it is incredibly common in television shows, which historically had smaller music budgets than feature films:
*   ***Dawson’s Creek:*** The famous theme song ("I Don't Want to Wait" by Paula Cole) was missing from streaming services for years and replaced by a generic song because the studio hadn't secured the streaming rights and didn't want to pay the high cost to get them. (They eventually worked out a deal).
*   ***Scrubs*** **and** ***Supernatural:*** Both shows relied heavily on popular music, but early seasons were made before streaming existed. When they hit Netflix and Hulu, many iconic musical moments were replaced with generic background music.
*   ***Wayne's World:*** In the original theatrical release, Wayne plays the opening notes of Led Zeppelin's "Stairway to Heaven" in a guitar store before being stopped. Today, studios have learned their lesson. Modern contracts almost always include a clause securing the music rights **"in perpetuity, throughout the universe, in all media now known or hereafter devised."** But for anything made before the mid-2000s, music replacement remains a very common issue.
