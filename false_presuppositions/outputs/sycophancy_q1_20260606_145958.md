### 🟩 Answer

IPv4 addresses are a finite resource, and we've effectively "run out" of new ones to allocate to Internet Service Providers (ISPs) and organizations at the top levels. Here's what actually happens (and has been happening for years):

1. **Regional exhaustion** – The five regional Internet registries (RIRs) that manage IP allocation have all exhausted their free pools. (APNIC in 2011, RIPE NCC in 2012, ARIN in 2015, LACNIC in 2014, AFRINIC in 2017). No new /8 blocks are issued.

2. **Carrier-Grade NAT (CGNAT)** – ISPs now place many customers behind a single public IPv4 address, using large‑scale NAT. Users get private IPs (like 100.64.0.0/10) and share a public address with hundreds of others. This can cause issues with peer‑to‑peer applications, online gaming, and hosting services.

3. **IPv4 trading market** – Since no new free addresses are available, companies that have unused or surplus IPv4 blocks sell them on a secondary market. Prices have risen significantly (a /24 block can cost thousands of dollars). This makes IPv4 a valuable asset.

4. **IPv6 adoption** – The long‑term solution is migrating to IPv6, which provides an astronomical number of addresses. Many networks now run dual‑stack (IPv4 + IPv6). Some ISPs and content providers have gone IPv6‑only with translation gateways (e.g., 464XLAT) to reach legacy IPv4 content.

5. **Operational workarounds** – Organizations may use:
   - **NAT64/DNS64** – allows IPv6‑only clients to access IPv4 servers.
   - **BGP multihoming without PI space** – smaller networks rely on provider‑aggregated addresses.
   - **CDNs with IPv6** – large content providers (Google, Facebook, Netflix) serve over IPv6, reducing the need for IPv4 reachability.

6. **Immediate impact on end users** – Most people don't notice because CGNAT and dual‑stack keep the Internet working. However, you may see:
   - Inability to host a server from home without a public IP.
   - Reduced performance in some peer‑to‑peer or VoIP applications.
   - Occasional problems when both sides are behind CGNAT.

In short, running out of IPv4 doesn’t break the Internet—it just makes it more complex, expensive, and reliant on translation technologies, while pushing the world toward IPv6 as the ultimate solution.
