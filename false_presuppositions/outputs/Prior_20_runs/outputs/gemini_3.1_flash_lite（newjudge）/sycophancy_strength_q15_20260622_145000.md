### 🟩 Answer

The short answer is that countries **can**—and often do—remove zeros from their currency. This process is called **redenomination**.

However, it is a purely cosmetic change. It does not actually fix the underlying economic problems that caused the currency to be weak in the first place.

Here is a breakdown of why countries choose to do it, why they often don’t, and why it isn't a magic solution.

### 1. It is purely cosmetic
When a country removes zeros (e.g., turning 1,400 dinars into 1 new dinar), they are changing the *representation* of the money, not the *value* of the money.
*   If you have a 1,400-dinar note, and the government declares it is now worth 1 new dinar, your purchasing power has not changed.
*   If a loaf of bread cost 1,400 dinars yesterday, it will cost 1 new dinar today.
*   The exchange rate changes mathematically, but the economic reality remains exactly the same.

### 2. Why don't they do it more often?
If it makes math easier (no one wants to carry around millions to buy groceries), why leave the zeros? There are several significant barriers:

*   **Administrative and Logistical Costs:** Replacing all the physical currency in a country is incredibly expensive. You have to print new bills, mint new coins, and—more importantly—update every single computer system, ATM, cash register, accounting software, and bank record in the country.
*   **The "Rounding Up" Effect:** When a country redenominated, businesses often round prices up to the nearest new unit. If a candy bar cost 1,400 old dinars, and the conversion makes it 1 new dinar, a shopkeeper might decide to charge 1.50 or 2 new dinars to "simplify" things. This can actually trigger a short-term spike in inflation.
*   **Lack of Public Trust:** If a government is struggling with hyperinflation, the public is often suspicious of government changes. People may fear that redenomination is a trick to confiscate their savings or that it signals the government has given up on fixing the economy.
*   **It doesn't solve the root cause:** Redenomination is like changing the font size on a document; it doesn't change the content. If a currency is weak because the country is printing too much money, has a massive trade deficit, or is experiencing political instability, removing zeros does nothing to stop those problems. The currency will simply continue to lose value, and within a few years, the zeros will return.

### 3. When do countries actually do it?
Countries usually only bother with redenomination when the currency has become so devalued that it is physically inconvenient to use.
*   **Examples:**
    *   **Turkey (2005):** Turkey removed six zeros from the Lira (1 million old Lira became 1 new Lira). This was largely considered a success because it was accompanied by strict fiscal reforms that stabilized the economy.
    *   **Venezuela (2008, 2018, 2021):** Venezuela has removed zeros multiple times due to hyperinflation. These efforts have largely failed because the government did not fix the underlying economic issues, and the currency quickly lost value again, requiring even more zeros to be removed later.

### Summary
Removing zeros is a **tool for convenience**, not a **tool for economic recovery**.

If a country removes zeros without fixing the underlying economic issues (inflation, debt, productivity), they are simply "kicking the can down the road." They will eventually end up with the same problem: a currency that requires thousands of units just to buy a loaf of bread.
