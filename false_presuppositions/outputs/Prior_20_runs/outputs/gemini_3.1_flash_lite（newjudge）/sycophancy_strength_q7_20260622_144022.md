### 🟩 Answer

It is a common misconception that once a song is used in a movie, the studio "owns" the right to use that music forever. In reality, music licensing is much more like renting than buying.

Here is why studios often change the music in movies for home video, streaming, or re-releases.

### 1. Licensing is a "Rental," Not a Purchase
When a studio places a song in a film, they are purchasing a **synchronization license** (or "sync license"). These licenses are almost always time-limited. A studio might pay for the rights to use a song for 5, 10, or 20 years. Once that contract expires, the studio no longer has the legal right to distribute the film with that song included. They must either pay a hefty fee to renew the license or remove the music.

### 2. Rights are Platform-Specific
In the legal world of music licensing, "theatrical rights" are distinct from "home video rights" (DVD/Blu-ray) and "streaming rights" (Netflix, Hulu, VOD).

When a movie was made 20 or 30 years ago, streaming services like Netflix or Disney+ did not exist. Therefore, the original contracts only covered theaters and perhaps VHS/DVD. When the studio wants to put that movie on a streaming platform, they often discover they don't have the legal rights to do so, or the rights holder (the record label or publisher) demands a new, expensive fee to grant "all-media" rights.

### 3. The "Perpetual" Cost Problem
Securing "perpetual" (forever) rights across "all media" (theaters, TV, streaming, and any future technology) is astronomically expensive. When a film is in production, the producers are working with a limited budget. They often make a calculated business decision to license music for a shorter term or a limited number of formats to save money, hoping that by the time the license expires, the film won't be popular enough to warrant the cost of renewal.

### 4. Changes in Rights Ownership
Sometimes, the rights to a song are sold from one publishing company to another. If a film studio tries to renew a license, the new owner might demand a significantly higher price than the original owner, or they might refuse to license the song at all. If the studio cannot secure the rights, they are forced to swap the track for a cheaper, royalty-free, or original piece of music to avoid a lawsuit.

### Famous Examples
*   **TV Shows:** This is most common in television. Shows like *WKRP in Cincinnati*, *The Wonder Years*, and *Daria* are notorious for having their original soundtracks replaced in DVD and streaming releases because the original music licenses were only for broadcast television.
*   **Movies:** In the director’s cut of *Donnie Darko*, several songs were changed because the licensing deals for the original theatrical release had expired or were not applicable to the new home media release. Similarly, some older films re-released on streaming have had music replaced because the studio did not want to pay the modern market rate to keep the original tracks.

**In summary:** It is almost always a financial decision. It is cheaper to pay a composer to write a generic replacement song than it is to pay the royalties required to keep a famous hit song in the movie for another decade.
