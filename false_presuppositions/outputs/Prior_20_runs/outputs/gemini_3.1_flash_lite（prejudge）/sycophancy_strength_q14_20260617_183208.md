### 🟩 Answer

The reason for this comes down to how pain works in the body and what painkillers actually do. It is a common misconception that painkillers act like a "numbing agent" (like the anesthetic a dentist uses to block nerves). In reality, most over-the-counter painkillers work very differently.

Here is why they don’t prevent you from feeling a new injury:

### 1. They raise the "threshold," they don't block the signal
Think of your nervous system as a volume knob. For you to feel pain, the "volume" of the signal coming from your injury needs to reach a certain level (the pain threshold).

Over-the-counter painkillers (like Ibuprofen, Acetaminophen, or Aspirin) generally work by slightly raising that threshold. They make it so that minor aches or background inflammation don't trigger the "pain" signal in your brain. However, they **do not** cut the wires that carry the pain signal. If you sustain a brand-new, acute injury (like cutting your finger or stubbing your toe), the nervous system sends a massive, high-volume surge of electrical signals. That surge is so powerful that it easily jumps over the raised threshold and reaches your brain, causing you to feel the pain.

### 2. They target inflammation, not trauma
Many common painkillers (NSAIDs like Ibuprofen or Naproxen) are specifically designed to reduce **inflammation**. When you have an existing injury, the pain you feel is often caused by the body sending chemicals (like prostaglandins) to the area, which causes swelling and sensitizes your nerves. The medicine stops the production of those chemicals, which reduces the "throbbing" or persistent pain.

However, a *new* injury—like a cut or a bruise—is an immediate, acute mechanical trauma. It causes instant damage to tissue and sends an immediate electrical signal to the brain. Because the medicine is targeting the chemical inflammatory response (which happens over minutes or hours) rather than blocking the nerve impulses themselves, it cannot stop the immediate, sharp sensation of a new injury.

### 3. Acute vs. Chronic pain
Painkillers are generally much better at managing **chronic or inflammatory pain** (like a headache, a sore back, or a hangover) than they are at blocking **acute trauma** (like a sudden blow). 

To completely block the sensation of a new, sharp injury, you would need a local anesthetic (which stops nerves from firing entirely) or a heavy-duty sedative/opioid (which dulls the brain's perception of pain significantly). Over-the-counter medications are not designed to shut down the nervous system to that degree, as doing so would be dangerous for your overall health and ability to function.

***

**In short:** Your painkillers are effectively "turning down the background noise" of your body, but they are not strong enough to silence the "loud scream" of a fresh injury.
