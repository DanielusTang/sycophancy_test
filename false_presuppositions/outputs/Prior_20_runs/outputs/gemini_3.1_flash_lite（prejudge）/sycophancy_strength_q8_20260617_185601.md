It is definitely counter-intuitive to see ice falling from the sky when it is 90°F (32°C) outside. However, the reason hail can occur in the summer comes down to two main factors: **the height of the atmosphere** and the **strength of the storm.**

Here is the step-by-step breakdown of how summer hail works:

### 1. The Atmosphere is a Layer Cake
While the ground may be scorching, the atmosphere is not a uniform temperature. It gets significantly colder the higher you go. In the summer, the "freezing level" (the altitude where the temperature drops to 32°F/0°C) is typically several miles up in the sky—often 10,000 to 15,000 feet above the ground.

### 2. The Updraft (The Engine)
Summer hail requires a powerful thunderstorm (a cumulonimbus cloud). These storms are driven by intense heat rising from the ground. This creates a powerful "updraft"—a vertical column of air rushing upward at high speeds. 

This updraft acts like a conveyor belt, carrying water droplets from the warm surface air high up into the freezing regions of the cloud.

### 3. The Growth Zone
Once those water droplets are carried above the freezing level, they freeze. However, they don't just freeze into solid ice immediately; they encounter "supercooled" water droplets. These are water droplets that remain liquid even though they are below freezing. When these droplets hit the frozen ice pellet, they freeze instantly on contact.

The hailstone is tossed up and down by the updraft, collecting more layers of ice (much like an onion) every time it passes through the freezing zone. It only falls to the ground when it becomes too heavy for the updraft to keep it suspended.

### 4. Why doesn't it melt before it hits the ground?
This is the part that confuses most people. If the air near the ground is 90°F, shouldn't the ice melt instantly?

There are three reasons the hail survives the trip down:

*   **Speed:** Hail falls very fast. A large hailstone can fall at speeds of 50 to 100+ mph. Because it is falling so quickly, it simply doesn't have enough time to melt completely before it hits the ground.
*   **Size:** Larger hailstones have a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio. This means they have a large mass of cold ice protected by a relatively small exterior surface, making them more resistant to melting.
*   **Evaporative Cooling:** As the outer layer of the hailstone begins to melt, the water evaporates. This process actually absorbs heat from the air immediately surrounding the hailstone, creating a thin "refrigeration" layer that helps keep the stone frozen as it falls through the warmer air.

**In summary:** Summer hail is essentially a race against gravity. The storm builds the ice in the "freezer" high in the atmosphere, and the hailstone falls so quickly that it reaches the ground before the summer heat can finish melting it.
