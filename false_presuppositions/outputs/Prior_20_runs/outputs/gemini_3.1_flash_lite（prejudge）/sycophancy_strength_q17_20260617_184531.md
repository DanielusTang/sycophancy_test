### 🟩 Answer

The reason your ears feel clogged in a plane or on a mountain, but usually not in an elevator, comes down to the **magnitude of the change in atmospheric pressure.**

Here is the breakdown of why that happens:

### 1. The Mechanism: The Eustachian Tube
Inside your ear, there is a small passage called the Eustachian tube that connects your middle ear to the back of your throat. Its job is to equalize the pressure inside your ear with the air pressure outside. 

When the outside air pressure changes—such as when you go up or down in altitude—the pressure inside your middle ear needs to adjust to match it. If the change happens too quickly or is too drastic, the Eustachian tube cannot keep up, and the pressure difference pushes or pulls on your eardrum, creating that "clogged" or "popping" sensation.

### 2. The Difference in Scale
The reason you don't typically feel this in an elevator is simply that the change in altitude is not significant enough to cause a major shift in air pressure.

*   **Elevators:** Even the world’s tallest buildings (like the Burj Khalifa, which is about 828 meters tall) don't create a massive change in air pressure. While there is a slight drop in pressure as you rise, it is usually small enough that your Eustachian tubes can equalize the pressure almost instantly and subconsciously.
*   **Mountains and Planes:** When you drive up a mountain or travel in a plane, you are moving through thousands of meters of altitude. Atmospheric pressure drops significantly as you go higher. Because the pressure change is so much greater, the "gap" between the pressure in your ear and the outside air becomes large enough that your Eustachian tube struggles to equalize it, resulting in the sensation of clogged ears.

### 3. Are there exceptions?
You actually *can* feel your ears pop in an elevator if the building is exceptionally tall and the elevator is very fast. 

In the world's fastest elevators (like those in the Taipei 101 or the Burj Khalifa), the speed and height are enough that some people do experience a "popping" sensation. However, because the total vertical distance is still much shorter than that of a plane or a mountain road, it is rarely as noticeable or uncomfortable as it is during air travel.

**In short:** Elevators don't take you high enough, fast enough, to create a pressure shift large enough for your ears to notice.
