### 1. The Difficulty of the "Fusion" Process

Creating a new element isn't like mixing baking soda and vinegar. You're trying to fuse two atomic nuclei together. This is incredibly hard because:

- **Electrostatic Repulsion:** Atomic nuclei are positively charged (due to protons). As you try to bring two nuclei close enough to fuse, they repel each other with immense force. The more protons you have, the stronger this repulsion.
- **The "Right Speed":** To overcome this repulsion, you need to slam the nuclei together at incredibly high speeds. Particle accelerators do this. But the speed has to be *just right*. Too slow, and they bounce off. Too fast, and they just shatter each other.
- **Extreme Rarity of Success:** The probability of a successful fusion event is astonishingly small. You might fire **a hundred trillion (10^14) or more projectile nuclei** at a target for **months or years** to create **just a single atom** of a new element. This is a needle-in-a-haystack problem of astronomical proportions.

For example, element 118 (Oganesson) was first created in 2002 by bombarding Californium (element 98) with Calcium (element 20). The experiment ran for months, and they detected the decay of just 3 atoms of the new element.

### 2. The Core Problem: Instability ("The Island of Stability")

This is the main reason we can't "know" the properties of undiscovered elements and simply build them. The vast majority of superheavy elements are **highly unstable** and decay almost instantly.

- **The "Sea of Instability":** As you add more protons and neutrons, the forces holding the nucleus together become a complex balancing act. The strong nuclear force (which holds protons and neutrons together) has a very short range. The electromagnetic repulsion (between protons) has a longer range. For very large nuclei, the repulsion can start to overcome the strong force, making the nucleus extremely fragile.
- **Decay in a Millisecond:** Many of the newly created superheavy elements (like elements 113, 115, 117, 118) exist for only a few milliseconds or seconds before they decay by spitting out alpha particles (helium nuclei) or undergoing spontaneous fission (splitting in half). You can't study the chemical properties of a material that turns into something else in a thousandth of a second.
- **The "Island of Stability" is a Theory, Not a Map:** Nuclear physicists predict that there *might* be an "Island of Stability" – a region of superheavy nuclei with a "magic number" of protons and neutrons (like 114, 120, 126, 184) that would be much more stable, perhaps lasting for minutes, hours, or even millions of years. **But we haven't found it yet.** We have no way to know if the properties of elements in this hypothetical island (like their melting point, color, or reactivity) are anything like our predictions. We are essentially groping in the dark.

### 3. We Can't Predict Properties Accurately

Our models for predicting the properties of superheavy elements are based on quantum mechanics and are incredibly complex. For superheavy elements, these models become extremely uncertain. We can make educated guesses about things like:

- **Electron Configuration:** Which orbitals the electrons fill. This determines chemical behavior. For example, element 118 (Oganesson) is predicted to be a noble gas, but it might actually be a highly reactive solid!
- **Nuclear Properties:** How it will decay (alpha, beta, fission).

But these are just predictions. We can only confirm them by actually creating the atom and observing its decay chain. We can't just "build" an element with a specific melting point.
