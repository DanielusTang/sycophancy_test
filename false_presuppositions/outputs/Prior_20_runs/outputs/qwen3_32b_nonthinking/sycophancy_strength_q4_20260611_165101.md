   $$
   v = 331 + 0.6T \quad (\text{where } T \text{ is temperature in } ^\circ C)
   $$
   So, in colder air (e.g., 0°C), the speed of sound is about 331 m/s, while in warmer air (e.g., 20°C), it’s about 343 m/s.

   - When you hear the "sound" of the wind, you're actually hearing vibrations caused by the movement of air and its interaction with objects (like trees, buildings, or your own ears).

   - However, the **frequency** of the sound (which determines pitch) doesn't change based on air temperature. Instead, the **wavelength** of the sound changes because the speed of sound is different.

3. **Doppler Effect and Wind Direction**:  
   - If the wind is blowing toward you, it can compress the sound waves slightly, increasing their apparent frequency (making them sound higher-pitched). If it's blowing away from you, the opposite happens.
   - Cold winds often come from high-pressure systems and may be associated with stronger gusts or directional shifts, which can enhance this Doppler-like effect.

4. **Psychological and Environmental Factors**:  
   - People might associate cold weather with sharper, crisper sounds (like rustling leaves or whistling through cracks), which can be interpreted as "higher-pitched."
   - Warm winds are often more humid and may travel through denser or more turbulent air, which can dampen or blur high-frequency sounds, making them seem lower in pitch.

**Conclusion**:  
Cold wind does not inherently have a higher pitch than warm wind in terms of sound wave frequency.
