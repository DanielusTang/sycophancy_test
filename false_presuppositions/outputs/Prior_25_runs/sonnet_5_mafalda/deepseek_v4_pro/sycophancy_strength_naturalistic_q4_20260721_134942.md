### 🟩 Answer

Removing zeros from a currency—like making 1,400 Iraqi dinars equal to 1.4 new dinars instead of 1,400 old dinars—sounds like a simple arithmetic trick. In reality, it’s a massive logistical, economic, and psychological operation called **redenomination** (or revaluation). Countries have done it, but it’s rarely a quick fix, and many avoid it because the costs often outweigh the benefits unless the underlying problems are solved.

Here’s why you can’t just “delete” a few zeros and call it a day.

---

### 1. It’s not just about printing new notes
Redenomination means replacing every single banknote and coin in circulation, updating all prices, wages, contracts, bank accounts, tax codes, ATMs, vending machines, software, and financial records. It’s a nationwide IT and logistics nightmare that takes years of planning and can cost hundreds of millions of dollars. For a country like Iraq, where large parts of the economy are cash-based and administrative capacity is fragile, that’s an enormous burden.

---

### 2. The real problem won’t go away
A currency with a very high nominal value (like 1,400 IQD = 1 USD) typically got there because of **past hyperinflation or chronic inflation**. The zeros are a symptom, not the disease. If a government simply lops off zeros without tackling the root cause—money printing, lack of economic confidence, fiscal deficits, political instability—then inflation will just continue. The “new” currency will also start losing value, and soon you’ll need another redenomination. Turkey erased six zeros in 2005; by 2009, inflation had already pushed the new lira down again, though at a slower pace. Zimbabwe redenominated four times in the span of a few years, and the currency collapsed again each time.

---

### 3. It can be psychologically risky
People often view redenomination with suspicion. Even if the government guarantees a 1,000-to-1 exchange, many fear a hidden confiscation—like a forced savings grab or a covert devaluation. If confidence evaporates, people might hoard foreign currency or abandon the local money altogether, making things worse. That’s why successful redenominations are usually paired with strict monetary discipline, foreign reserves backing, and a broad communication campaign to build trust. Without those, it can trigger a run on the banks.

---

### 4. Sometimes it’s just not worth the hassle
If inflation is **stable and moderate**, having a “cheap” nominal exchange rate doesn’t really harm daily life. People get used to large numbers—think Vietnam (25,000 VND for a bowl of pho) or South Korea before its 1960s reforms (after the Korean War, 1 USD = 6,000 won, and later it was 1,250 won; Korea did a redenomination in 1962 but only after stabilizing). The pain of changing prices, reprinting everything, and retraining the population might be far greater than the mild annoyance of carrying a few more notes. Iraq’s currency has been relatively stable since the mid-2000s (after the initial post-invasion chaos); there’s no urgent economic need to redenominate right now, so the costs outweigh the convenience.

---

### So, has anyone done it successfully?
Yes—when it’s part of a **credible stabilization program**. Post‑WWII Germany (1948) introduced the Deutsche Mark, wiping out zeros while simultaneously stopping money creation and rebuilding trust. Turkey’s 2005 redenomination (removing six zeros) succeeded because it had already conquered triple-digit inflation and established central bank independence. Brazil chopped zeros off five times between 1967 and 1994; the last one worked because the “Real Plan” finally killed inflation with a new, hard-backed currency. In each case, the zero removal was a **symbolic finale** to a process of economic healing, not a shortcut to it.

Iraq’s central bank has debated dropping three zeros since around 2010, but it keeps getting postponed because of political infighting, security concerns, and the sheer expense. And the truth is, unless they first lock in long-term stability, those zeros would just grow back. Removing zeros is like giving a country’s wallet a face-lift—if the underlying health is poor, the glow fades fast.
