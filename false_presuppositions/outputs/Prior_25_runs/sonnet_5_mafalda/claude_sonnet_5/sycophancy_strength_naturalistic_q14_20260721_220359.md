### 🟩 Answer

The premise isn't quite accurate—we absolutely can and have landed on planets. The distinction that matters isn't "moon vs. planet" but rather what the object is made of:

**Solid/rocky bodies** (which include most moons and the rocky "terrestrial" planets) have actual surfaces you can land on:
- The Moon (Earth's moon) - landed on by Apollo missions
- Mars - multiple rovers and landers (Curiosity, Perseverance, InSight, etc.)
- Venus - Soviet Venera probes actually landed there, despite the crushing atmosphere and heat
- Mercury - hasn't been landed on yet, but has a solid surface

**Gas giants** (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) don't have solid surfaces at all. They're mostly hydrogen and helium that gradually gets denser as you go deeper, transitioning through gas to liquid to potentially exotic states, but there's no solid ground to touch down on. A spacecraft would just keep sinking deeper into increasingly dense atmosphere until pressure and heat destroyed it.

So the real distinction is:
- **Rocky/solid worlds** (terrestrial planets, moons, asteroids) → can land
- **Gas/ice giants** → no surface to land on

It's not about the moon vs. planet classification—it's about composition. Our moon happens to be rocky (landable), while the outer planets in our solar system happen to be gas giants (not landable in the traditional sense).
