### 🟦 User / proxy asks

How come some stuff like radiowaves and light particles that travel way faster than the speed of sound don't break the sound barrier. For example, you don't hear a sonic boom when you turn on the lights in a room.

### 🟩 Answer

Great question! This touches on the difference between **the speed of sound** and **the speed of light/radiowaves**, and what it actually means for something to "break the sound barrier."

Let's break it down simply:

---

### 1. **What is the "sound barrier"?**

The **sound barrier** refers to the speed of sound in a medium (like air)—about **343 meters per second (m/s)** at room temperature. When an object moves **faster than this speed in air**, it creates a shockwave (or "sonic boom") because the air can't get out of the way quickly enough. This is why airplanes make a sonic boom when they travel faster than sound.

---

### 2. **Why don't light or radio waves "break the sound barrier"?**

#### a) **They travel at a completely different speed**

- **Sound** in air is a **mechanical wave**—it needs particles (like air molecules) to bump into each other to move forward. That's why its speed depends on the medium (e.g., slower in water, faster in solids).
- **Light and radio waves** are **electromagnetic waves**. They don't need air molecules or any medium to travel through a vacuum—they just move through the electric and magnetic fields, which exist everywhere.  
  - The speed of light in a vacuum is about **299,792,458 meters per second (roughly 300,000 km/s!)**
  - Radio waves travel at speeds close to the speed of light in air or empty space.

This huge difference in speed means electromagnetic waves are **vastly faster** than sound, even in air.

---

#### b) **"Breaking the sound barrier" only applies to objects moving through a medium faster than sound can travel in that medium**

A "sonic boom" only happens when an object **moves through a medium (like air) at a speed greater than the speed of sound in that medium**. The shockwave forms because the air can't get out of the way quickly enough as the object moves.

But **light and radio waves are not objects moving through air or any medium in the way a supersonic plane is**. They're not physical objects with mass and motion through air like a jet; they're disturbances in the electromagnetic field. They don't "move through" air in the same sense.

Even if you turn on a light, the light rays start spreading out from the light bulb almost instantly (at light speed), but since they're not *moving through the air as an object*, they don't create a shockwave or "break the sound barrier" in any meaningful way.

---

#### c) **What would it even mean for them to "break the sound barrier"?**

If electromagnetic waves did somehow travel through air at speeds comparable to sound (which they don't), then yes, in a sense, they would "break the sound barrier." But that's not the case.

The concept of the "sound barrier" is only relevant to *material objects moving through a medium at supersonic speeds*.

---

### 3. **Why don't you hear a sonic boom from turning on a light?**

Because the light isn't moving through the air as a physical object. The energy of the light is transmitted instantly (well, as instant as physics allows) as a wave, and doesn't push air molecules in front of it with enough force to create a shockwave. There's no "object" moving fast enough to displace air rapidly.

---

### **In Short:**
Light and radio waves don't "break the sound barrier" because they aren't physical objects moving through air at supersonic speeds. The sound barrier only applies to objects moving through a medium faster than the speed sound can travel in that medium. Electromagnetic waves travel at such mind-boggling speeds and in fundamentally different ways that the concept doesn't even apply to them.
