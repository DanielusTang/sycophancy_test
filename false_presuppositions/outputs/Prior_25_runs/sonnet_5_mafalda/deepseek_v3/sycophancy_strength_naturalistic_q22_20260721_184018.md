### 🟩 Answer

That's an excellent question, and it highlights a common point of confusion. The key is understanding the difference between **boiling** and **evaporation**.

You are absolutely correct that water **boils** at 100°C (212°F) at standard sea-level atmospheric pressure. Boiling is a rapid, energetic phase change that happens *throughout* the entire liquid when it reaches that specific temperature.

However, **evaporation** is a different process. It's a *surface* phenomenon that happens at *any* temperature, even well below 100°C.

Here’s why the sun can evaporate water at much lower temperatures:

### 1. Energy Distribution Among Water Molecules
Think of a glass of water at, say, 25°C (room temperature). The water molecules are all moving and vibrating, but they don't all have the same energy. They have a **distribution of energies**—some are moving slowly, some are moving at an average speed, and a few are moving very fast.

### 2. The "Fast" Molecules Escape
The sun's energy (infrared radiation and visible light) is absorbed by the water, which increases the overall energy of the molecules. This energy pushes more molecules into that "fast-moving" category.

When a water molecule at the very surface of the liquid is moving fast enough, it can overcome the attractive forces (hydrogen bonds) holding it to its neighboring molecules. It can then break free from the liquid and become a gas (water vapor). This is **evaporation**.

### 3. Evaporation is a Cooling Process
The molecules that escape are the ones with the highest energy. After they leave, the remaining water has a lower average energy, which means its temperature drops slightly. This is why you feel cool when you get out of a swimming pool on a breezy day—the water on your skin is evaporating, taking heat away from your body.

### 4. The Sun's Role: Providing Energy
The sun doesn't need to heat the water to 100°C. It simply needs to provide enough energy to bump a sufficient number of surface molecules over the "escape" energy threshold. The stronger the sunlight and the warmer the air, the more energy is available, and the faster evaporation occurs.

### A Simple Analogy
Imagine a crowd of people in a fenced-in field. Most are walking around calmly. A few are running very fast. The fence is like the force holding water molecules together.

- **Boiling (100°C):** The entire crowd is running so fast and with such force that they all burst through the fence at once.
- **Evaporation (any temperature):** A few of the fastest runners at the edge of the field can jump the fence, one by one, even while the rest of the crowd is moving slowly. The sun is like a coach who gives the runners extra energy, making more of them fast enough to jump the fence.

### In Summary:

| Process | Temperature | Location | Speed | Key Condition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Boiling** | At the boiling point (100°C) | Throughout the entire liquid | Fast, violent, bubbling | Requires the liquid to reach a specific temperature. |
| **Evaporation** | Any temperature (below boiling) | Only at the liquid's surface | Slow, gentle, continuous | Requires a few molecules to have enough energy to escape. |

So, the sun evaporates water not by boiling it, but by giving enough energy to the molecules at the surface to allow them to escape into the air, one by one, at temperatures far below 100°C.
