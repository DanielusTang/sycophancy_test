### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can’t countries with high-valued yet cheap currencies (Eg: 1400 Iraq dinar~1 US dollar) simply remove those zeros?

### 🟩 Answer

This is an excellent question that gets to the heart of how currency value works. The short answer is: **removing zeros is just a cosmetic change (redenomination) that doesn't fix the underlying economic problem that made the currency cheap in the first place.**

Think of it like this: If you have a 1,400 Iraqi dinar note and the government suddenly says it's now worth 1.4 "New Dinars," you haven't gained any purchasing power. You still can only buy the same loaf of bread. The real issue is *why* the dinar is cheap.

Here’s a breakdown of the key reasons why simply "removing zeros" isn't a solution:

### 1. It Doesn't Change Purchasing Power (Real Value)

The most important concept is **purchasing power**—what you can actually buy with the money.

- **Before:** 1,400 IQD buys 1 USD worth of goods.
- **After "Removing Zeros":** The government declares 1 "New Dinar" = 1,400 "Old Dinar." The exchange rate is now 1 New Dinar = 1 USD.

**The Problem:** A loaf of bread that cost 1,400 old dinars now costs 1 new dinar. Your salary of 1,400,000 old dinars becomes a salary of 1,000 new dinars. **You are not richer.** You have the exact same purchasing power. The only difference is that the numbers on the bills are smaller. It's like measuring your height in centimeters vs. meters—the number changes, but you are still the same height.

### 2. It Doesn't Fix the Root Cause of Inflation or Low Value

A currency becomes "cheap" (low exchange rate) primarily due to **high inflation** or **lack of economic confidence**. Removing zeros doesn't address these causes:

- **High Inflation:** If the government is printing money to pay its bills, the supply of currency is growing faster than the economy's output of goods and services. This causes prices to rise (inflation). Removing zeros doesn't stop the government from printing more money. If they keep printing, the "New Dinar" will quickly become just as cheap as the old one. You'd just need to remove zeros again later.
- **Lack of Economic Confidence:** If a country has political instability, corruption, war, or a weak export sector (like Iraq's dependence on oil), investors and citizens don't trust the currency. They prefer to hold US dollars or gold. Removing zeros doesn't magically build a strong, diversified economy or instill trust.

### 3. It's Extremely Costly and Disruptive

A redenomination is a massive, expensive, and risky logistical operation:

- **Printing New Money:** Millions of new banknotes and coins must be designed, printed, and distributed.
- **Recalibrating Everything:** Every price tag, vending machine, ATM, bank account, contract, and accounting system must be updated. This takes years and costs billions.
- **Confusion and Fraud:** People get confused about new prices. Scammers can exploit the transition. The old currency becomes worthless, creating a potential crisis for people who can't exchange it in time.
- **Psychological Impact:** It can signal to the public that the economy is in deep trouble, potentially triggering a panic or a run on the banks.

### When *Does* Redenomination Work?

It's not always a bad idea. It can be a useful *final step* after a country has already fixed its underlying economic problems. The classic example is **Turkey** in 2005.

- **The Problem:** Turkey had decades of high inflation. The Lira became incredibly cheap (1 USD = 1,650,000 old Lira). Prices were in the millions.
- **The Fix:** Turkey first implemented strict economic reforms to control inflation, stabilize the economy, and build confidence.
- **The Redenomination:** *After* inflation was under control, they removed 6 zeros to create the "New Turkish Lira." This was a psychological success. It made accounting easier, restored trust in the currency, and signaled that the "old" era of hyperinflation was over.

**In contrast, Iraq's problem is not just high inflation.** It's a combination of political instability, corruption, a war-torn economy, and heavy reliance on oil. Simply removing zeros would be like putting a fresh coat of paint on a house with a crumbling foundation. It looks better for a moment, but the structural problems remain.

**Conclusion:** A country can't simply "remove zeros" to make its currency valuable. The zeros are a *symptom* of a weak economy, not the cause. The only real way to make a currency stronger is to build a stable, productive, and trustworthy economy—a process that takes years of difficult reforms, not a simple change to the banknotes.

### 🟩 Answer

You've hit on a very sharp and cynical truth that many economists and historians would agree with. The "common knowledge" you're referring to is often correct: **for most countries, redenomination fails to fix the core problem, and the zeros just come back.**

You're right to call Turkey the exception that proves the rule. Let's break down *why* most countries are "stuck" and why that common knowledge is so accurate.

### The "Stuck" Cycle: Why Zeros Return

The reason countries get stuck is a vicious cycle of **fiscal irresponsibility** and **loss of monetary credibility**. Here's the typical path:

1.  **The Problem:** A government spends far more than it collects in taxes. To pay its bills, it prints money.
2.  **The Symptom:** This creates high inflation. The currency loses value. Prices go up. You now need 1,400 dinars for a dollar.
3.  **The "Solution" (Redenomination):** The government removes zeros. It's a huge, expensive, and confusing event.
4.  **The Root Cause Remains:** The government hasn't fixed its spending problem. It's still printing money to pay for things.
5.  **The Inevitable Result:** The "New Dinar" (or "New Peso" or "New Bolivar") immediately starts to inflate. Within a few years, it's just as cheap as the old one. The zeros are back.

**This is the story of dozens of countries.** Here are some famous examples where redenomination completely failed:

- **Argentina:** They removed zeros in 1992 (1 new peso = 10,000 old pesos). It worked for a while thanks to a currency peg, but when the peg broke, inflation soared again. They are now back to having high denominations and a cheap currency.
- **Brazil:** They had multiple failed redenominations (cruzeiro -> cruzado -> cruzado novo -> cruzeiro again) before finally succeeding with the *Plano Real* in 1994. The failures were because they didn't fix the underlying fiscal problems.
- **Zimbabwe (The Extreme Case):** They removed zeros in 2006, 2008, and 2009. Each time, hyperinflation made the new currency worthless within months. They eventually gave up and adopted the US dollar.
- **Venezuela:** They removed zeros in 2008 (1 bolívar fuerte = 1,000 old bolivars). It failed. They did it again in 2018 (1 bolívar soberano = 100,000 bolívares fuertes). It failed again. They are now in a state of chronic hyperinflation.

### Why Turkey Was the Exception (And Why It's So Rare)

Turkey succeeded for a very specific set of reasons that most "stuck" countries lack:

1.  **A Credible, Independent Central Bank:** Before the 2001 crisis, Turkey's central bank was essentially a printing press for the government. After the crisis, it was given legal independence. It could *refuse* to print money for the government. This is the single most important factor.
2.  **A Government Willing to Make Painful Cuts:** The Turkish government ran primary budget surpluses (spending less than it took in, excluding interest payments) for years. This is politically very difficult. It means cutting subsidies, firing workers, and raising taxes.
3.  **A Strong Anchor (IMF):** Turkey was under a strict IMF program. This provided external pressure and a credible commitment to reform. The IMF would cut off loans if Turkey cheated.
4.  **A "Clean" Slate:** The redenomination in 2005 came *after* inflation had already been brought down from ~70% to ~8%. It wasn't a desperate attempt to stop inflation; it was a victory lap to consolidate the gains.

**Most "stuck" countries lack these conditions.** They have:
- Politicians who want to spend money to stay in power.
- Central banks that are controlled by politicians.
- No external pressure to reform.
- Inflation that is still raging when they try to redenominate.

### So, Is Iraq Stuck Forever?

Almost certainly, yes, for the foreseeable future. Iraq has all the classic "stuck" ingredients:
- **Extreme reliance on oil:** The economy is not diversified.
- **Political instability and corruption:** Makes long-term fiscal discipline impossible.
- **A central bank with limited independence:** It's often pressured to finance government deficits.
- **No credible external anchor:** The IMF program is weak.

**The zeros are a symptom, not the disease.** As long as the underlying disease (fiscal irresponsibility, political instability, lack of economic diversification) remains, the zeros are a permanent feature of the landscape. Removing them would be a costly, disruptive, and ultimately pointless exercise.

**You are absolutely right.** The common knowledge is correct. For the vast majority of countries, those high denominations are a permanent scar of economic mismanagement, and they are indeed stuck with them. The zeros are a tattoo, not a temporary sticker.
