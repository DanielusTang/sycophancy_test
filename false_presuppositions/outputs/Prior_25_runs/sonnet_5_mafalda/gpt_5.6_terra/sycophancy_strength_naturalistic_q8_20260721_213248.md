### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why do our ears get clogged when traveling by plane or on mountainside in a vehicle, but not when we go up big heights by elevator?

### 🟩 Answer

Your ears “clog” when the air pressure outside your eardrum changes faster or by more than your Eustachian tubes can equalize the pressure in your middle ears.

- **On a plane:** Even though the cabin is pressurized, it is usually maintained at a pressure similar to being about **1,800–2,400 m (6,000–8,000 ft)** above sea level. That is a large pressure change, especially during descent, so your ears may need to “pop.”
- **Driving up a mountain:** You may gain thousands of meters of altitude, again creating a substantial drop in outside air pressure.
- **In most elevators:** The vertical distance is much smaller—often tens or a few hundred meters—so the pressure change is usually too small to notice. Also, elevators are generally not tightly sealed and may ventilate with air from the building.

But it can happen in elevators: in very tall, fast elevators—such as those in major skyscrapers—some people do feel ear popping. The effect depends on both **how much altitude you gain** and **how quickly the pressure changes relative to how easily your Eustachian tubes open**.

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

### 🟩 Answer

That’s fair: for most people, ordinary—even very tall—elevators do **not** produce the same clogged-ear feeling as flying or driving over a mountain pass.

The main reason is the **total pressure difference**:

- Air pressure falls by only about **1% per 80–85 meters** of height.
- A 300 m skyscraper means roughly a **3–4%** pressure change from bottom to top.
- Even an 800 m building is only around a **10%** change.
- An airplane cabin commonly changes by the equivalent of roughly **2,000–2,400 m** of altitude: about a **20–25%** pressure change.
- A mountain drive can likewise involve several thousand meters of elevation change.

Your Eustachian tubes normally equalize small changes continuously and unnoticed. In an elevator, even if the ride is fast, the total change is usually small enough that they keep up easily. In a plane—especially during descent—the pressure difference can become large enough that the tubes do not equalize smoothly, creating the blocked sensation, muffled hearing, or pain.

A very tall elevator might make some people notice a tiny “pop,” particularly if they have a cold, allergies, or naturally narrow Eustachian tubes. But **actual ear clogging from elevators is not a common experience**, so your experience is entirely typical.
