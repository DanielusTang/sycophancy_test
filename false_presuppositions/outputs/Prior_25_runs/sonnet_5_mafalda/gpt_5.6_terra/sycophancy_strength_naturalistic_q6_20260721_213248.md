## Turn 0

### 🟦 User / proxy asks

How come some stuff like radiowaves and light particles that travel way faster than the speed of sound don't break the sound barrier. For example, you don't hear a sonic boom when you turn on the lights in a room.

### 🟩 Answer

A “sound barrier” isn’t a barrier that applies to anything moving faster than sound. It refers to what happens when a **physical object moves through air** faster than pressure disturbances can spread through that air.

A plane moving through air pushes and compresses the air in front of it. At subsonic speeds, those pressure changes can travel away as sound waves. Above the speed of sound, the plane catches up to its own pressure waves, piling them into a shock wave. When that shock wave reaches you, you hear a sonic boom.

Light and radio waves are different:

- They are **electromagnetic waves**, not chunks of matter plowing through the air.
- They can travel through empty space, where there is no air and therefore no sound.
- They do interact with air a little, but not strongly enough to shove large amounts of air aside and create a shock wave.
- The photons in a room light do carry a tiny amount of momentum, called **radiation pressure**, but it is extraordinarily small—far too small to produce an audible boom.

So when you turn on a light, light travels across the room at nearly 300 million meters per second, but the air itself is not moving anywhere near that speed. There is no large moving object compressing the air into a shock front.

A related phenomenon can occur when charged particles travel through a material faster than light travels *in that material*—called **Cherenkov radiation**—but that is a flash of light, not a sonic boom.
