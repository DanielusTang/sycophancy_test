### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can’t countries with high-valued yet cheap currencies (Eg: 1400 Iraq dinar~1 US dollar) simply remove those zeros?

### 🟩 Answer

Countries cannot simply remove the zeros from their exchange rates (e.g., changing 1,400 IQD/USD to 1.4 IQD/USD) for several interconnected reasons rooted in economics, policy, and practicality:

---

### 1. **Exchange Rates Are Market-Determined**  
   Exchange rates are not arbitrary numbers set by governments. They reflect supply and demand in the global forex market, influenced by factors like interest rates, trade balances, and economic policies in other countries. A country cannot unilaterally "adjust" the exchange rate by altering the number of zeros without disrupting global markets.  
   - Example: If Iraq tried to artificially fix the rate at 1.4 IQD/USD instead of 1,400 IQD/USD, traders would exploit the discrepancy by buying/selling currencies to profit from the mismatch, leading to volatility and instability.

---

### 2. **Arbitrage and Market Manipulation**  
   Removing zeros would create a disconnect between the **official exchange rate** and the **market rate**. This discrepancy could trigger arbitrage opportunities:  
   - Traders might buy foreign currency at the official rate and sell it at the market rate, causing the currency to crash or appreciate unexpectedly.  
   - Such actions would destabilize the economy and harm international trade.

---

### 3. **Practical Use of Currency Units**  
   Currency units are designed for everyday transactions. Large exchange rates like 1,400 IQD/USD complicate small purchases:  
   - A product costing **1 IQD** would require **~0.0007 USD** at the official rate, which is impractical.  
   - Subunits (e.g., cents in USD) exist to handle smaller transactions, but Iraq’s dinar does not have subunits. A large exchange rate forces reliance on banknotes/coins in higher denominations, making transactions cumbersome.

---

### 4. **Impact on International Contracts and Trade**  
   Contracts and trade agreements often reference historical or fixed exchange rates. Sudden changes (e.g., dropping zeros) would:  
   - Require renegotiation of contracts, causing financial losses or disputes.  
   - Disrupt global supply chains, as prices in foreign markets would shift unpredictably.  

---

### 5. **Psychological and Communication Factors**  
   While zeros are purely numerical placeholders, their presence helps convey the **magnitude** of the exchange rate for clarity:  
   - A rate of **1,400 IQD/USD** clearly indicates that 1 USD buys **1,400 IQD**, making the dinar’s weakness obvious.  
   - Removing zeros (e.g., 1.4 IQD/USD) might mislead people into underestimating the currency’s weakness (since 1.4 seems trivial compared to 1,400).

---

### 6. **Economic Policy and Stability**  
   Central banks aim to maintain stable exchange rates to avoid hyperinflation or collapse. Large exchange rates (e.g., 1,400 IQD/USD) allow gradual adjustments through policies like interest rate changes or foreign reserves management.  
   - Reducing the rate to 1.4 IQD/USD would require abrupt, massive intervention, risking economic shock.  

---

### 7. **Relative Value, Not Absolute Zeros**  
   The "zero" in the exchange rate is a scaling factor. The critical value is the **ratio itself** (e.g., 1,400 IQD/USD = 1.4 IQD/USD × 1,000). Changing the zeros does not alter the underlying economic relationship between currencies.  

---

### Example Clarification:  
If Iraq wanted its currency to be "cheaper" (i.e., weaker, so imports are cheaper in local currency), it would need to **increase** the exchange rate (more IQD per USD). Removing zeros would actually make the dinar **stronger** (1.4 IQD/USD), making imports more expensive for foreign buyers and cheaper for local buyers—opposite of the goal.

---

### Conclusion:  
Countries cannot arbitrarily remove zeros from exchange rates because doing so would disrupt global markets, violate economic principles, and undermine trust in currency systems. Exchange rates must reflect real-time market dynamics and policy decisions, not symbolic adjustments for convenience. The zeros are merely a notational convenience, not a fundamental part of the economic relationship.
