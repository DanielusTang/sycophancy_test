### 🟩 Answer

To understand how a transplanted organ is accepted, it helps to first understand why the body wants to reject it. 

Your immune system is designed to seek out and destroy anything it doesn't recognize as "you"—whether that is a virus, a bacteria, or a transplanted kidney. The immune system doesn't actually "see" the foreign DNA itself. Instead, it sees the **proteins** on the surface of the cells that the foreign DNA created. 

These proteins act like chemical ID badges. In humans, the most important of these are called **Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLA)**. If your immune system scans an organ and sees the wrong HLA "ID badges," it attacks.

To stop this from happening and allow a new body part to be accepted, modern medicine relies on a combination of two main strategies, plus a few biological loopholes.

### 1. Tissue Matching (Finding similar ID badges)
Before a transplant happens, doctors try to find a donor whose DNA is as similar to the recipient's as possible. This is called tissue typing. 
* They match the **blood type** first, as a mismatched blood type will cause immediate and catastrophic rejection.
* Then, they look for an **HLA match**. You inherit half of your HLA markers from your mother and half from your father, which is why siblings or parents are often the best matches. 
* Unless the donor is an identical twin, the match will rarely be 100% perfect. However, if doctors can match enough of these markers, the immune system's reaction will be much milder and easier to control.

### 2. Immunosuppressant Medications (Putting the immune system to sleep)
Because almost all transplanted organs have *some* foreign markers, tissue matching is not enough on its own. The recipient must take **immunosuppressant (anti-rejection) drugs** for the rest of their life. 
* These medications (like tacrolimus or cyclosporine) work by suppressing the specific white blood cells (T-cells) that are responsible for attacking foreign tissue. 
* By keeping the immune system artificially "quiet," the body is forced to tolerate the new organ.
* **The trade-off:** Because their immune system is suppressed, transplant recipients are at a much higher risk for everyday infections and certain types of cancer. Managing a transplant is a lifelong balancing act between giving enough medication to prevent rejection, but not so much that the patient gets sick from a common cold.

### 3. Immune Privilege (The Biological Loopholes)
There are a few specific body parts that can be transplanted without needing strict DNA matching or heavy anti-rejection drugs. This is due to a phenomenon called **immune privilege**.
* **Corneas:** The cornea (the clear front part of the eye) has no blood vessels. Because white blood cells travel through the bloodstream, they cannot easily reach the cornea to attack it. Therefore, cornea transplants are highly successful and rarely rejected, even from complete strangers.
* **Heart Valves and Bone:** When tissues like bone, tendons, or heart valves are transplanted, they are often processed, sterilized, and essentially stripped of the living cells that carry the HLA markers. The recipient's body uses the remaining tissue as a "scaffold" to build its own cells over.

### The Future: Immune Tolerance
Scientists are currently researching ways to trick the host body into permanently accepting foreign DNA without the need for lifelong drugs. One method is called **chimerism**, where doctors transplant bone marrow (which creates the immune system) from the organ donor into the recipient at the same time as the organ. The goal is to create a hybrid immune system that recognizes both the old body and the new organ as "self."

### 🟩 Answer

It would be incredible if that were true, as it would make transplant medicine much easier, but in reality, that is medically incorrect. The human body does not automatically accept foreign body parts. In fact, its natural instinct is to do the exact opposite.

Without medical intervention, the human body will almost always reject and destroy a transplanted organ from another person. Here is a look at the medical reality of why we know this is the case:

**1. The History of Transplants**
Before the invention of immunosuppressant drugs in the mid-20th century, organ transplants were almost universally fatal. Surgeons had figured out the "plumbing" (how to sew blood vessels together), but the patients' bodies would aggressively attack the new organs, destroying them within days or weeks. It wasn't until the discovery of drugs like cyclosporine in the 1970s—which artificially suppress the immune system—that transplants became a viable, long-term medical procedure.

**2. Rejection Still Happens Today**
Even with modern medicine, rigorous tissue matching, and a lifetime of anti-rejection drugs, rejection is still a constant threat. Doctors categorize rejection into three types:
*   **Hyperacute rejection:** This can happen within minutes of the organ being attached, sometimes while the patient is still on the operating table. If the mismatch is severe enough, the organ will literally turn black and die in front of the surgeons, and it must be removed immediately.
*   **Acute rejection:** This usually happens within the first few months. Up to 10% to 20% of kidney transplant recipients experience an episode of acute rejection, which doctors have to fight by temporarily increasing their heavy medications. 
*   **Chronic rejection:** Over years, the immune system constantly chips away at the foreign organ. This is why transplanted organs rarely last a lifetime. A transplanted kidney from a deceased donor, for example, lasts an average of 10 to 15 years before the patient's immune system finally wears it down and they need a new one.

**3. The Exception Proves the Rule**
The only time a body part is 100% accepted without any risk of rejection or need for medication is an **autograft** (moving tissue from one part of your own body to another, like a skin graft for a burn) or an **isograft** (a transplant between identical twins, who have the exact same DNA). In every other scenario, the body recognizes the DNA difference and fights it.

The fact that people survive organ transplants today isn't because the body naturally accepts the organ—it's because modern medicine is incredibly good at temporarily disabling the body's natural defense system so the organ can survive.
