### 🟩 Answer

The reason oceans and seas do not act as giant spreaders of human viruses and contagious illnesses comes down to a combination of chemical, physical, and biological factors that make the marine environment incredibly hostile to human pathogens. 

Here are the primary reasons oceans keep these illnesses in check:

**1. Extreme Dilution**
For a person to become infected by a virus or bacteria, they must be exposed to a minimum amount of the pathogen, known as the "infectious dose." The ocean is so unfathomably massive that any human pathogens introduced into it (such as through sewage) are rapidly diluted. The concentration drops so low that it becomes highly unlikely for a person to encounter a high enough dose to get sick.

**2. High Salinity (Saltwater)**
Human bodies are about 0.9% salt, and the pathogens that infect us are adapted to that specific environment. The ocean has a salinity of about 3.5%. When terrestrial bacteria and viruses enter the ocean, the high salt concentration triggers a process called *osmosis*. The salt draws the water out of the pathogen's cells or viral envelopes, effectively dehydrating and destroying them.

**3. Temperature Shock**
Most viruses and bacteria that cause contagious illnesses in humans thrive at our internal body temperature (98.6°F / 37°C). The vast majority of the ocean is much colder than this. The cold water shocks these pathogens, severely limiting their ability to survive, metabolize, or remain structurally intact for very long.

**4. Ultraviolet (UV) Radiation**
Sunlight acts as a massive, natural disinfectant. As pathogens float near the surface of the ocean, they are bombarded by UV radiation from the sun. UV light damages the DNA and RNA of viruses and bacteria, rendering them unable to replicate or infect a host. 

**5. Biological Competition and Predation**
The ocean is not sterile; it is teeming with its own microscopic life. When human pathogens enter the water, they are outcompeted for nutrients by native marine microbes. Furthermore, the ocean is full of filter feeders (like oysters, clams, and sponges) and microscopic predators (like bacteriophages—viruses that specifically hunt and kill bacteria) that consume and destroy terrestrial pathogens.

**6. Host Specificity**
Viruses cannot reproduce on their own; they need a host cell. Furthermore, viruses are usually highly specific to the species they infect. The viruses that naturally exist in the ocean are adapted to infect marine life (like phytoplankton, fish, and whales), not humans. Conversely, human viruses cannot use marine life to replicate. Without a human host nearby, a human virus in the ocean will simply degrade over time.

**The Exception: Coastal Pollution**
While the open ocean is highly effective at destroying human illnesses, **coastal waters can sometimes be an exception.** After heavy rainfall, agricultural runoff and overflowing sewage systems can dump massive, concentrated amounts of bacteria (like *E. coli*) and viruses (like Norovirus) into shallow beach waters. Because these waters are shallow, warmer, and closer to humans, the dilution and temperature factors are reduced, which is why beaches are sometimes closed to swimming after major storms to prevent illness. Additionally, there are a few naturally occurring marine bacteria (like *Vibrio*, sometimes called "flesh-eating bacteria") that thrive in warm, brackish coastal waters and can infect humans, though they are not contagious from person to person.
