### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can’t countries with high-valued yet cheap currencies (Eg: 1400 Iraq dinar~1 US dollar) simply remove those zeros?

### 🟩 Answer

This is an excellent and intuitive question. The idea of simply "removing zeros" (a process called **redenomination**) sounds like a quick fix, but it's far more complex and risky than it appears. The core reason is that **removing zeros doesn't change the underlying economic reality** that made the currency "cheap" in the first place.

Let's break down why it's not a simple solution, using your example of the Iraqi Dinar (IQD).

### The Fundamental Misunderstanding: Value vs. Denomination

The key is to distinguish between a currency's **nominal value** (the number on the bill) and its **purchasing power** (what you can actually buy with it).

- **Cheap Currency (Low Purchasing Power):** 1,400 IQD buys you a single US dollar. This means the purchasing power of one IQD is very low. The root cause is usually high inflation, economic instability, or lack of confidence.
- **High Nominal Value (Many Zeros):** The large number of zeros is a *symptom* of this low purchasing power, not the cause.

**Simply removing zeros is like changing the scale on a broken thermometer.** If a room is 100°F, you can't make it 72°F by erasing the "1" and the first "0" and writing a "7" and a "2". The actual temperature (the economic heat) remains the same.

### What Happens When You "Remove Zeros" (Redenomination)?

Let's imagine Iraq redenominates its currency, removing three zeros. The new 1 Dinar note replaces the old 1,000 Dinar note.

**The Official Exchange Rate Changes:**
- **Before:** 1,400 IQD = 1 USD
- **After:** 1.4 New IQD = 1 USD

On paper, the exchange rate looks much better! But this is only an accounting change. The government must also re-price everything in the economy.

**The Critical Problem: What Happens to Prices?**

The government would decree that:
- A loaf of bread that cost 2,000 old IQD now costs **2 new IQD**.
- A car that cost 14,000,000 old IQD now costs **14,000 new IQD**.
- Your salary of 1,400,000 old IQD becomes **1,400 new IQD**.

Your **purchasing power remains exactly the same**. You can still buy the same loaf of bread and the same car. The number of zeros in your wallet and on the price tag has changed, but the *real value* hasn't.

### So, Why Don't They Just Do It? The Real-World Risks

If it's just an accounting change, why is it so difficult? Because it's not just an accounting change. It's a massive, risky logistical and psychological operation.

1.  **Massive Logistical Nightmare:** Every single price tag, contract, bank account, ATM, vending machine, accounting software, and cash register in the entire country must be updated simultaneously. This is incredibly expensive and prone to error. For a country with a less developed digital infrastructure, this is a herculean task.

2.  **The "Window of Chaos" for Inflation:** This is the biggest danger. During the transition period, there is immense confusion. Businesses might use the confusion to round prices *up* in their favor.
    - **Example:** A loaf of bread was 1,500 old IQD. The official conversion is 1.5 new IQD. But a baker, worried about future costs, might round it up to **2 new IQD**. This is a 33% price increase in real terms! This can trigger a new wave of inflation, undoing any potential benefit.

3.  **Loss of Confidence & Potential for Capital Flight:** Redenomination is often seen as a sign of desperation. Citizens and foreign investors might interpret it as "the government has given up on fixing the economy." This can cause a panic, where people rush to convert their savings into US dollars or gold (capital flight), further weakening the new currency.

4.  **It Doesn't Solve the Root Problem:** The core issue is why the currency became "cheap" in the first place. Was it because the government printed too much money to pay its bills (inflation)? Was it because the economy is unproductive and relies too heavily on oil? Was it because of corruption and lack of rule of law?
    - **Redenomination does nothing to fix these problems.** If the government continues to print money recklessly, the new "zero-less" currency will just start inflating again, and in a few years, it will have its own set of zeros.

### When *Does* Redenomination Work?

It *can* be successful, but only as the *final step* after the underlying economic problems have been solved. The classic example is **Turkey in 2005**.

- **The Problem:** Turkey had suffered from decades of high inflation. The Lira had lost so much value that a single US dollar was worth over 1.5 million old Lira. Prices were in the millions and billions.
- **The Solution:** Turkey first implemented a strict, credible economic program to crush inflation. They stabilized prices and the exchange rate. *Then*, after several years of low, stable inflation, they redenominated, removing six zeros (1 New Lira = 1,000,000 Old Lira).
- **Why it worked:** The hard work of fixing the economy was already done. The redenomination was just a cosmetic cleanup, making accounting easier and restoring psychological confidence. It was the ribbon on a finished package, not the construction itself.

### Conclusion for Iraq and Similar Cases

For Iraq, simply removing zeros would be a dangerous and pointless exercise. The fundamental issues are:

- **Economic instability** (reliance on oil, conflict, corruption).
- **Lack of confidence** in the government and central bank.
- **The black market** for dollars, which operates at a different rate than the official one.

Until these problems are addressed, the Iraqi Dinar will remain "cheap" regardless of how many zeros are on the banknotes. The zeros are just a number; the real value is determined by the health of the economy. Removing them would be like putting a fresh coat of paint on a house with a crumbling foundation.
