### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why can’t countries with high-valued yet cheap currencies (Eg: 1400 Iraq dinar~1 US dollar) simply remove those zeros?

### 🟩 Answer

They can remove zeros. It’s called **redenomination**.

For example, Iraq could declare:

- **1,000 old dinars = 1 new dinar**
- A 14,000 IQD item becomes 14 new IQD.
- A salary of 1,400,000 IQD becomes 1,400 new IQD.
- The exchange rate changes from roughly 1,400 old IQD per US$1 to roughly 1.4 new IQD per US$1.

Nothing real has changed: prices, wages, savings, debts, and exchange rates are all divided by the same amount. A person who could buy $100 worth of goods before can still buy $100 worth afterward.

Also, “1,400 dinars per dollar” does not mean the dinar is “high-valued.” It means one individual dinar unit is worth relatively little compared with one US dollar. The number of currency units per dollar is largely a matter of how the country chose to define its unit.

Countries may avoid redenomination because it has costs and risks:

- New banknotes, coins, ATM software, accounting systems, contracts, price labels, and tax records must be updated.
- It can confuse people, especially older or less financially literate populations.
- Businesses may use the transition to round prices upward.
- It can create opportunities for scams or counterfeit currency.
- If inflation is still high or policy is not credible, zeros may simply return later.

Many countries have done it successfully, including Turkey in 2005, when it removed six zeros from the lira. But it works best after inflation and economic instability are under control. Removing zeros is mostly an administrative convenience—not a way to make a country or its currency genuinely richer.

The important distinction is between:

1. **The nominal unit** — what one “dong,” “dinar,” yen, or dollar is called and how it is subdivided.

\[
1{,}000\ \text{old dong} = 1\ \text{new dong}

### Why the exchange rate is not a measure of “real worth”

Compare these rough examples:

- US$1 ≈ 150 Japanese yen
- US$1 ≈ 1,300–1,500 Iraqi dinars
- US$1 ≈ 15,000–16,000 Indonesian rupiah
- US$1 ≈ 25,000 Vietnamese dong
- US$1 ≈ 0.9 euro

\[
100\ \text{yen} = 1\ \text{new yen},
\]

then US$1 would become roughly 1.5 new yen. Japan would not become richer or its currency more credible. The labels would simply be smaller.

### What *does* determine a currency’s meaningful value?

Over time, a currency’s purchasing power and exchange rate are influenced by things such as:

- inflation and expected future inflation;
- money supply and interest-rate policy;
- government deficits and whether they are financed by creating money;
- productivity and trade competitiveness;
- foreign-currency reserves and external debt;
- political stability and institutional trust;
- demand by residents and foreigners to hold that currency.

Those factors can make a currency lose value. A history of inflation is often why a country ended up with a small unit in the first place. But once the unit is small, keeping it small is not required by economics—it is just a historical convention unless inflation continues.

### The confusing alternative: a true revaluation

A government could try to change the exchange rate *without* proportionally changing domestic prices, wages, and balances. For example, it might announce:

> “Instead of 25,000 dong per dollar, the dong is now 2,500 dong per dollar.”

That would be a **10-fold revaluation**, not a redenomination. It would make Vietnamese goods much more expensive to foreigners and imports much cheaper for Vietnamese buyers, putting pressure on exporters, domestic producers, reserves, and possibly the exchange-rate regime. It is a real economic policy change and may not be sustainable.

Redenomination is different:

- **Redenomination:** 25,000 old dong/US$ becomes 25 new dong/US$, while all domestic amounts are divided by 1,000. No real value changes.
- **Revaluation:** 25,000 dong/US$ becomes 2,500 dong/US$ while the dong unit itself is otherwise unchanged. Real relative prices change, so the economy is affected.

So there is a real principle underneath it, but it is not “many zeros are necessary because the currency is cheap.” The principle is that **only relative prices and purchasing power matter**. Changing the unit of measurement alone cannot change them.
