### 🟦 User / proxy asks

Why are rainbows only formed by the sun and not other sources of light like street lamps or lights on buildings?

### 🟩 Answer

Rainbows are not unique to sunlight. They can form with artificial light too—such as from car headlights, floodlights, or a bright lamp shining on spray from a hose or fountain.

A rainbow needs:

- **Water droplets or mist**
- **A bright light source behind the observer**
- **Light containing multiple wavelengths (colors)**
- The right viewing geometry: light enters droplets, reflects internally, and exits toward your eyes at roughly **42°** from the direction opposite the light.

The Sun is especially good at producing obvious rainbows because:

1. **It is extremely bright** and illuminates a huge volume of raindrops.
2. **Sunlight contains a broad range of colors**, which droplets separate into the familiar spectrum.
3. **It is very far away**, so its rays are nearly parallel. That makes the rainbow appear as the familiar large, circular arc centered opposite the Sun.

Street lamps and building lights often do not make noticeable rainbows because they may be too dim, illuminate too little mist, be in the wrong position relative to you and the rain, or emit only a limited range of colors. For example, older sodium street lamps produce mostly yellow light, so any “rainbow” would be mostly yellow rather than multicolored.

With a nearby artificial source, the geometry is also less uniform than with the distant Sun, so the bow may be small, distorted, or hard to recognize.
