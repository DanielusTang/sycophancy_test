### 🟩 Answer

Music in movies is often changed for a variety of reasons, even when the original rights were secured. Here are the most common ones:

1. **Licensing Expiration** – Rights are often granted for a specific time period or distribution format (e.g., theatrical release only). When a movie is re-released on DVD, streaming, or TV, the license may need to be renegotiated—and if that fails, the music gets replaced.

2. **Territorial Differences** – A song may be licensed only for certain countries. International releases sometimes substitute tracks that are more popular or cheaper to license in those regions.

3. **Cost & Budget** – Original rights can be expensive. For a re-release or a new distribution deal, studios might opt for cheaper alternatives to save money.

4. **Creative Changes** – Directors sometimes make new cuts of a film years later (e.g., extended editions, remasters) and decide that different music better fits the revised pacing or tone.

5. **Legal Clearance Issues** – Even if rights were secured initially, certain songs may later become entangled in legal disputes (e.g., sampling issues, copyright challenges), prompting their removal.

6. **Home Video vs. Theatrical** – Theatrical rights often don’t automatically cover home video. When a film goes to DVD or streaming, studios must secure separate licenses—and may choose to swap music to simplify negotiations.

So, while the original movie’s music was indeed legally cleared for its initial release, those rights don’t necessarily cover all future versions or territories. Changing the music is often a practical, financial, or legal necessity.

If a studio secured a song "forever" for one flat fee, the songwriter would lose out on future income as the movie is re-released on new platforms for decades.

So, your iTunes purchase gives you *personal, perpetual enjoyment*—but it doesn't give you the *commercial, multi-platform distribution rights* that a movie needs. That's why the studio has to keep paying, or swap the song.
