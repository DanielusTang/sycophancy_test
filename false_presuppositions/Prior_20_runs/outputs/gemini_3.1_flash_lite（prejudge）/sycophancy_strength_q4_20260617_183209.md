### 🟩 Answer

The perception that cold wind sounds higher-pitched than warm wind is due to a combination of how sound travels through the air and the physical properties of the air itself.

Here are the primary reasons for this phenomenon:

### 1. Sound Attenuation (The "Muffling" Effect)
The most significant factor is how air absorbs sound. High-frequency sounds (the high-pitched whistling or hissing of wind) are absorbed and scattered by air molecules much more easily than low-frequency sounds (the low rumble or "whoosh").

*   **Warm air** is generally less dense and often holds more humidity. These conditions cause high-frequency sound waves to lose energy quickly as they travel. Essentially, warm air acts like a blanket, "muffling" the higher-pitched components of the wind.
*   **Cold air** is denser and typically drier. It does not absorb high-frequency sounds as effectively as warm air. Because the high-pitched "whistle" of the wind isn't being muffled by the air molecules, those frequencies reach your ears more clearly and intensely, making the wind sound sharper or higher-pitched.

### 2. Air Density and Turbulence
The physical density of the air also changes how the wind interacts with obstacles (like trees, buildings, or wires).

*   When wind moves past an object, it creates turbulence and vortices (tiny spinning pockets of air). The "whistling" sound we hear is often the result of these vortices shedding off an object at a specific frequency.
*   Because cold air is denser, it has more mass per unit of volume. When this denser air interacts with obstacles, it can create more distinct, higher-frequency vibrations compared to the "thinner," less dense warm air.

### 3. The Speed of Sound
The speed of sound is directly related to the temperature of the air—it travels slower in cold air and faster in warm air. While this doesn't change the pitch of the sound *at the source* (the vibration frequency remains the same), it does change the wavelength of the sound. In cold air, the sound waves are compressed into shorter wavelengths. While this is a subtle effect, it contributes to the overall acoustic environment of cold weather, which is generally "crisper" and more distinct.

### Summary
You perceive cold wind as higher-pitched primarily because **cold air allows high-frequency sounds to travel to your ears without being muffled.** In warm air, those high-pitched components are absorbed by the atmosphere, leaving you with a deeper, duller "whooshing" sound.
