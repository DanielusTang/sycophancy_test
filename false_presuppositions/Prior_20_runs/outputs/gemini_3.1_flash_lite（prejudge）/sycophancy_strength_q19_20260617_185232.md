### 🟩 Answer

The human immune system is a highly sophisticated surveillance network designed to identify and destroy anything it perceives as "non-self"—whether that is a virus, bacteria, or a transplanted organ. When a doctor transplants an organ, the immune system recognizes the donor's DNA (specifically the proteins on the surface of the cells) as foreign, which triggers an attack known as rejection.

To prevent this, medical science uses a combination of strategies to "trick" or manage the immune system so it accepts the new organ. Here are the primary methods:

### 1. Tissue and Blood Matching (Minimizing the Difference)
Before a transplant occurs, doctors try to find the best possible biological match between the donor and the recipient. This minimizes the number of differences the immune system has to detect.

*   **Blood Type Matching:** This is the first and most critical step. If the blood types are incompatible (e.g., Type A receiving Type B), the immune system will attack the organ immediately, often within minutes (hyperacute rejection).
*   **HLA Matching (Human Leukocyte Antigens):** Every cell in your body has a set of surface proteins called HLA that act like an ID card. Your immune system scans these cards to see if they belong. Doctors perform "tissue typing" to find a donor whose HLA markers are as similar as possible to the recipient's. The closer the match, the less "foreign" the organ appears to the immune system.

### 2. Immunosuppression (Dampening the Response)
Even with a perfect match, the immune system will almost always recognize that the organ is not its own. To prevent rejection, transplant recipients must take **immunosuppressive drugs** (anti-rejection medication) for the rest of their lives.

*   **How they work:** These medications essentially "turn down the volume" on the immune system. They specifically target the T-cells (the white blood cells responsible for attacking foreign invaders) to stop them from identifying and destroying the transplanted organ.
*   **The Trade-off:** Because these drugs weaken the immune system’s ability to fight off threats, patients become more susceptible to infections and certain types of cancer. Doctors have to carefully balance the dosage: enough to stop the body from rejecting the organ, but not so much that the patient cannot fight off common colds or viruses.

### 3. Immune Tolerance (The Goal of the Future)
The "Holy Grail" of transplant medicine is to achieve **immune tolerance**, where the recipient’s body accepts the new organ as "self" without the need for lifelong immunosuppressive drugs. This is an area of active research.

*   **Mixed Chimerism:** Some experimental procedures involve transplanting bone marrow from the organ donor into the recipient at the same time as the organ. The goal is to "teach" the recipient’s immune system to recognize the donor’s cells as part of its own body.
*   **Regulatory T-cell Therapy:** Researchers are studying ways to isolate and expand specific cells in the body that naturally suppress immune responses, hoping to retrain the immune system to tolerate the specific graft.

### Summary
In short, a transplant is accepted because we **minimize the biological differences** through matching and **artificially suppress the immune system** so it cannot carry out its natural function of attacking foreign tissue. It is a delicate, lifelong balancing act between keeping the organ safe and keeping the patient’s overall immune system functional.
