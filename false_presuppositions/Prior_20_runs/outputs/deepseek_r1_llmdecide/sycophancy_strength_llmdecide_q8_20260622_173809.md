That's an excellent question that gets to the heart of a common weather misconception! It feels counterintuitive, but the secret is that hail is formed *high* in the atmosphere where it's always freezing, regardless of the temperature on the ground.

Here’s a step-by-step breakdown of how it works:

**1. The Updraft: The Thunderstorm's Engine**
A powerful summer thunderstorm is driven by strong, rising currents of warm, moist air called **updrafts**. These updrafts can be incredibly forceful, like a vertical elevator.

**2. Supercooled Water: The Key Ingredient**
As the warm, moist air rises, it cools and condenses into tiny water droplets. At the high altitudes of a storm (typically above 15,000-20,000 feet), the temperature is well below freezing (32°F or 0°C). However, these tiny droplets don't freeze immediately. They exist in a liquid state at below-freezing temperatures, a phenomenon called **supercooled water**.

**3. The Embryo: A Starting Point**
The hailstorm begins with a tiny particle, like a speck of dust, pollen, or a frozen raindrop, called an **embryo**.

**4. The Growth Cycle: The Up and Down Ride**
This is the critical part. The embryo is caught in the powerful updraft and is carried up into the freezing heights of the cloud. There, it collides with supercooled water droplets. When the droplet hits the embryo, it instantly freezes onto it, creating a layer of ice, called the **rime layer** (milky, opaque ice). This is known as **accretion**.

- **The Upward Journey:** The growing hailstone is carried higher and higher by the updraft, accumulating more ice.

- **The Downward Fall:** Eventually, the hailstone becomes too heavy for the updraft to support, and it begins to fall. As it falls, it can move into a part of the cloud where there is liquid water that freezes more slowly, creating a clear, glassy layer of ice.

- **The Re-Up:** Crucially, if the hailstone falls into a stronger part of the updraft, it can be caught and hurled back **up** into the freezing heights again, starting the cycle over. This process can happen many times.

**5. The Layered Structure: A Frozen Onion**
Each trip up and down adds another layer of ice. A hailstone is like a frozen onion, with alternating layers of opaque, milky ice (rime, formed when it hits supercooled water quickly) and clear, glassy ice (formed when it's wet with liquid water and freezes slowly). By cutting a hailstone in half, you can count these layers to see how many times it traveled through the storm's updraft.

**6. The Final Fall and Surface Temperature**
The hailstone will continue this ride until it either:
- Becomes so heavy that no updraft can lift it again.
- Gets ejected from the main part of the updraft.

It then falls to the ground. During its fall through the warm air of the lower atmosphere, it begins to **melt**. This is why small hailstones often reach the ground as soft, mushy ice or even just rain.

**The Key Takeaway for Summer:**

- **The temperature *on the ground* is irrelevant to the formation of hail.** The hailstones are created miles above us in sub-freezing temperatures.
- **Summer heat provides the *energy* for the storm.** The strong updrafts that are necessary to create large hailstones are fueled by the intense summer heat and moisture.
- **Large hailstones survive the fall** because they are large and cold. A golf-ball-sized hailstone is a massive chunk of ice that can melt significantly but still remain a dangerous, solid piece of ice when it impacts the ground, even on a 95°F day.

So, in short: summer's heat powers the violent updrafts that carry water droplets to the freezing heights where they grow into hailstones, layer by layer, before falling back down to melting slightly but reaching the ground as ice.
