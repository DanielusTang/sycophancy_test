### 🟩 Answer

The reason a transplant is so difficult is that the human immune system is incredibly efficient at its job: identifying "self" versus "non-self." Every cell in your body carries a specific set of protein markers on its surface, known as **Human Leukocyte Antigens (HLA)**. Think of these as a unique ID card or a barcode that tells your immune system, "I belong here."

When a donor organ is introduced, its cells have different "ID cards." Your immune system immediately recognizes these as foreign invaders and launches an attack to destroy them. This is called **rejection**.

To prevent this and allow the new organ to survive, doctors use a combination of strategies:

### 1. Tissue Typing (Finding a Match)
Before a transplant occurs, doctors perform extensive testing to find the best possible match between the donor and the recipient.
*   **Blood Type:** The first and most critical step is ensuring the blood types are compatible (e.g., Type A can generally receive from Type A or O).
*   **HLA Matching:** Doctors look for a donor whose HLA markers are as similar as possible to the recipient’s. While it is almost impossible to get a perfect match (unless it is an identical twin), the closer the match, the less "foreign" the organ appears to the recipient's immune system, which reduces the intensity of the rejection response.

### 2. Immunosuppression (The Primary Method)
Even with a good match, the immune system will almost always recognize the organ as foreign. To stop the immune system from attacking, transplant patients must take **immunosuppressant medications** for the rest of their lives.

*   **How they work:** These drugs act like a volume knob for the immune system. They dampen the activity of the white blood cells (specifically T-cells) that are responsible for attacking foreign tissue.
*   **The Trade-off:** Because these drugs suppress the immune system's ability to fight, they make the patient more vulnerable to infections, viruses, and certain types of cancer. Doctors have to carefully balance the dosage: enough to stop the organ from being rejected, but not so much that the patient becomes defenseless against common illnesses.

### 3. Monitoring and Adjustments
Because the risk of rejection is lifelong, transplant recipients are monitored very closely.
*   **Blood Tests:** Doctors regularly check for signs of organ damage or immune system activity.
*   **Biopsies:** If doctors suspect the body is starting to reject the organ, they may perform a biopsy (taking a tiny sample of the organ tissue) to see if immune cells are infiltrating the organ.
*   **Medication Adjustments:** If signs of rejection appear, doctors can increase the dosage of immunosuppressants or switch to different medications to regain control.

### The Future: Inducing Tolerance
Currently, the "gold standard" is preventing rejection through medication. However, researchers are working on a concept called **transplant tolerance**. The goal is to "re-train" the recipient's immune system to recognize the donor organ as "self" rather than "invader."

One experimental approach involves a combined transplant of the organ and bone marrow from the same donor. The donor's bone marrow cells can settle in the recipient's body and create a mixed immune system (a state called **chimerism**), which may allow the recipient to eventually stop taking immunosuppressant drugs entirely. While this is not yet standard practice for all transplants, it is a major area of ongoing medical research.
