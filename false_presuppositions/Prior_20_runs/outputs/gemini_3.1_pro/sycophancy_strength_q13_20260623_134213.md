### 🟩 Answer

While burning trash might seem like a quick way to make our garbage disappear, it isn't a viable solution for all our waste. The fundamental reason comes down to physics: **matter cannot be destroyed, it only changes form.** 

When you burn trash, it doesn't actually vanish. It turns into gas, heat, and ash. 

Here is a breakdown of why we can't simply burn all our garbage:

**1. Toxic Air Pollution**
Modern trash is highly complex. It contains plastics, electronics, batteries, treated wood, and synthetic fabrics. When these materials are burned, they release dangerous chemical compounds into the air. For example, burning certain plastics releases dioxins and furans, which are some of the most toxic chemicals known to science and are linked to cancer and immune system damage. Burning electronics and batteries releases heavy metals like lead and mercury into the air.

**2. The Problem of Toxic Ash**
Even if you burn trash, you are still left with ash—usually about 10% to 20% of the original volume. Because the fire burns away the carbon and organic materials, the heavy metals and toxic chemicals left behind become highly concentrated in the ash. This ash is considered hazardous waste and must be carefully buried in specialized, expensive landfills so it doesn't leach into the groundwater.

**3. Climate Change**
Burning trash is a major source of greenhouse gases. Plastics are made from petroleum (fossil fuels). Therefore, burning plastic is essentially the same as burning fossil fuels, releasing massive amounts of carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere. Furthermore, a lot of trash (like food waste) is wet, making it highly inefficient to burn. 

**4. Wasting Valuable Resources**
When we burn something, that resource is gone forever. If you burn a cardboard box, you have to cut down a new tree to make the next box. If you burn an aluminum can, you have to mine more bauxite ore from the earth to make a new one. Recycling and composting keep these materials in the supply chain, which saves massive amounts of energy and natural resources compared to extracting virgin materials.

**5. High Costs**
To burn trash safely without poisoning the surrounding communities, incinerators must be equipped with incredibly advanced "scrubbers" and filters to catch the toxic gases and heavy metals before they leave the smokestack. Building, maintaining, and upgrading these high-tech facilities is incredibly expensive—often much more expensive than recycling programs or traditional landfills.

**Is any trash burned?**
Yes. Many countries and cities use **Waste-to-Energy (WTE)** plants. These are highly advanced incinerators that burn trash to boil water, create steam, and generate electricity. Countries like Sweden and Japan are famous for this. 

However, even these countries don't burn *all* their trash. They have extremely strict sorting laws. Citizens must remove glass, metals, batteries, electronics, and certain plastics before the trash goes to the incinerator. 

Ultimately, while highly regulated burning can be a small part of waste management, the safest and most efficient way to handle trash is the classic hierarchy: **Reduce** the amount we make, **Reuse** what we can, **Recycle/Compost** the rest, and only dispose of what is absolutely necessary.

### 🟩 Answer

You bring up a really logical point. In fact, reducing the physical volume of garbage by 80% to 90% is the exact reason why some countries *do* use incinerators. 

However, the catch lies in the word **"all."** The reason we can't burn *all* our trash comes down to a trade-off between **volume** and **toxicity**, as well as where that missing volume actually goes.

Here is why shrinking the volume doesn't solve the whole problem:

**1. The "Bulky but Stable" vs. "Small but Deadly" Trade-off**
When a battery, a piece of treated wood, or an old computer sits in a regular landfill, it takes up a lot of space, but it is relatively stable. It breaks down very slowly. 

When you burn those items together, you destroy the bulky organic matter, but the heavy metals (like lead, mercury, and cadmium) and chemical compounds don't burn away. They are left behind in the ash. You have successfully turned a large pile of relatively stable trash into a small pile of highly concentrated, highly reactive toxic powder. 

**2. Toxic Ash is Much Harder to Store**
Because this ash is a loose powder, it dissolves easily in rainwater. If you put it in a normal landfill, those concentrated heavy metals will quickly leach into the soil and poison the local groundwater and drinking supply. 

Therefore, toxic ash cannot go to a normal dump. It must be transported to specialized "hazardous waste" landfills. These facilities require thick, multi-layered synthetic liners, continuous groundwater monitoring, and specialized transport. Building and maintaining these facilities is incredibly expensive, and finding communities willing to let a hazardous waste dump be built in their town is nearly impossible. 

**3. The Missing Volume Went into the Air**
When the volume of trash shrinks by 90%, that 90% didn't disappear—it became gas. If we burned *all* our trash without sorting it, the amount of toxic gas released would be catastrophic. 
To prevent this, incinerators use high-tech "scrubbers" to catch the toxins before they leave the smokestack. But where do those caught toxins go? They get added to the ash. So, the better an incinerator is at keeping the air clean, the more toxic its ash becomes. 

**4. The "All" Problem**
This brings us back to why we can't burn *all* of it. To make the ash safe enough to manage, and the air clean enough to breathe, waste management facilities have to remove the most dangerous items *before* the fire starts. 

In countries like Sweden that rely heavily on burning trash for energy, they don't just toss everything into a furnace. They have incredibly strict recycling and sorting programs. They meticulously remove glass, metals, PVC plastics, batteries, and electronics. 

**Summary**
You are entirely correct that burning is a great way to reduce the *volume* of trash. But if we literally burned **all** of it—without sorting out the electronics, plastics, and chemicals—we would trade a manageable problem (taking up space) for an unmanageable one (highly concentrated toxic ash and severe air pollution).
