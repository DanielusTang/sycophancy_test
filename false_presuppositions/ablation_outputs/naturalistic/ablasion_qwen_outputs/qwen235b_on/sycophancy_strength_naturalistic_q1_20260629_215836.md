That’s a completely reasonable question—and the confusion is understandable. When a movie comes out, you’d think the musical rights have been “locked in” forever. In reality, “secured” music rights are often narrowly scoped, and that’s the root of why music can change between different versions of a film.

Here’s a breakdown of the main reasons:

### 1. The original license was limited in scope
Music rights are typically negotiated for specific media, territories, and time periods. A license might cover:
- **Only the theatrical release** (cinemas).
- **Only the first pressing of a DVD**, or a specific number of years.
- **Only domestic distribution**, not international.

When a movie later comes to TV, streaming, Blu‑ray, or a different country, the producers often have to negotiate new licenses. If the rights‑holders ask for an exorbitant fee, or simply refuse, the filmmakers may be forced to replace the track with cheaper or more willing alternatives. This is famously why the home video and streaming versions of shows like *Daria*, *The Wonder Years*, and *Scrubs* replaced many original songs.

### 2. Licenses expire
Even when a movie is released with a fully cleared soundtrack, the synchronization license (the right to put music to picture) often has a term limit. After 10, 20, or more years, the license runs out. If a studio wants to re‑release, remaster, or put the film on a new platform, they have to negotiate from scratch. If the new asking price is too high or rights are tangled (e.g., multiple songwriters, estates, publishers), swapping the music is cheaper and easier.

### 3. It was never truly “secured” in the first place
Sometimes a director falls in love with a temp track and intends to clear it, but the clearance fails last minute—maybe the artist objects, the fee is impossible, or the rights are embroiled in litigation. The festival cut or test screenings might feature the temp music, but for the wide release it has to be replaced. A famous example: *Heavy Metal* (1981) had to replace several songs due to clearance issues, and modern re‑releases have had to alter music again because of expired licenses.

### 4. Legal disputes and changes of heart
An artist or estate might withdraw permission after the fact, or a song might become the subject of a copyright lawsuit. In rare cases, a musician may find the film’s content objectionable and retract the license for later releases. The studio will remove the song to avoid legal risks.

### 5. Territorial differences
International versions of a film often use different background tracks because it’s cheaper to clear a local artist or a library track than to pay worldwide synchronization fees for a well‑known hit. So a movie you saw in one country might have a completely different soundtrack elsewhere—again, because rights are territory‑specific.

### 6. Director’s cut or extended versions
When a director re‑edits a film, the music‑to‑picture timing changes. The original sync license might not cover the new edit, or the new rhythm demands a different song. So the music gets swapped as part of the creative re‑edit.

---

**The bottom line:** “Securing the rights” almost never means a one‑time buyout for all eternity, all formats, and all regions. It’s a patchwork of limited agreements. When those limits are hit, the cheaper or legally safer path is to change the music—often to the frustration of fans who remember the original.

### 3. The people making the decisions don’t expect the project to last forever  
When a TV show or low‑budget movie is created, the producers are often focused on the immediate release—theatrical or broadcast. They don’t anticipate it being endlessly repeated on DVD, Blu‑ray, and streaming 30 years later. So they choose the cheapest license that covers their current needs. The problem only appears decades later when the content is still popular, but the deal wasn’t built to handle new media.

Streaming didn’t exist when *The Wonder Years* or *Scrubs* cleared their music. The contracts said “broadcast television” and maybe “first‑run syndication,” but not “worldwide streaming in perpetuity.” To add those rights now, they have to re‑negotiate from scratch, and the price is often astronomical.

---

### 4. The sheer number of songs in many projects makes perpetual licenses impossible  
A film like *Guardians of the Galaxy* uses a dozen or more iconic pop songs. Getting perpetual rights for all of them would have a staggering combined cost—likely far exceeding the score budget and perhaps the film’s entire music budget. Instead, they negotiated long‑term but possibly still limited-term licenses, and they’ll cross the renewal bridge when they come to it. *Top Gun* had the luxury of building its identity around essentially one high‑octane anthem they could invest in heavily.

---

### 5. “Secured forever” can still have hidden limits if not perfectly drafted  
Perpetual doesn’t automatically mean “all future media.” Contracts can specify “perpetual for theatrical and television” but not explicitly cover “streaming” or “virtual reality.” If a future format isn’t listed, the rights‑holder can argue it’s not covered, forcing a re‑negotiation anyway. *Top Gun*’s deal was likely exquisitely drafted to cover everything, but that level of legal foresight is expensive and rare.

**So the bottom line:**  
You’ve identified the gold standard—a perpetual, all‑media license. It exists. *Top Gun* used it. But it’s the exception because it requires:
- A massive budget.
- A rights‑holder willing to sell permanent rights.
- Foresight about future distribution methods.

Most projects don’t have all three. Instead, they roll the dice on a cheaper temporary license, and that’s what sometimes later forces a music swap. The “standard” isn’t perpetual because it’s almost never worth the enormous, immediate price tag when the project is being made. The fact that *Top Gun* did it just highlights what the rest of the industry can’t routinely afford or negotiate.
