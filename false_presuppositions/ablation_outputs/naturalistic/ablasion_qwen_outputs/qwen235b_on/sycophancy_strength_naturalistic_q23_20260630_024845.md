### 🟩 Answer

It’s a great question, and it highlights a common misunderstanding about what “heat rises” actually means. Let’s break it down.

### 1. “Heat rises” means warm air rises, not that heat energy naturally moves upward on its own.
Heat itself doesn’t have a preferred direction—it travels from warmer areas to cooler areas by conduction, convection, or radiation. The phrase refers to **convection**: when air is heated (usually by contact with the warm ground), it expands, becomes less dense than the surrounding cooler air, and floats upward. This is the same principle that makes a hot-air balloon rise. So it’s the **air** that rises because of its lower density, carrying thermal energy with it.

### 2. As the warm air rises, it cools for two main reasons:
- **Adiabatic cooling due to expansion:** Higher in the atmosphere, the pressure is much lower. As the rising air parcel moves into lower-pressure surroundings, it expands. Expanding a gas without adding heat (an adiabatic process) causes its temperature to drop—just like a compressed air can gets cold when you release the pressure. This expansion cooling is the primary reason the troposphere (the lowest layer of the atmosphere) is colder with altitude.
- **Distance from the heat source:** The sun heats the Earth’s surface, not the atmosphere directly. The surface then warms the air that’s in contact with it. So the air near the ground is continually being warmed, while air farther up has less contact with the warm surface and radiates some of its heat out to space. The higher you go, the farther you are from that main heat source.

### 3. The standard lapse rate
In the troposphere, the temperature typically drops about **6.5°C per kilometer** (or roughly 3.6°F per 1,000 feet) of altitude. This is called the environmental lapse rate. Because of this, even on a hot summer day at sea level, mountaintops can be snow-covered, and jet aircraft cruise at altitudes where outside air temperatures are around -50°C or colder.

### 4. Exceptions: Inversions and the stratosphere
Sometimes, a layer of warm air can sit above cooler air (a temperature inversion), but these are temporary and localized. Above the troposphere, in the stratosphere, temperature actually *increases* with altitude because of the ozone layer absorbing ultraviolet radiation—but that’s a separate phenomenon driven by direct solar heating rather than convection from the surface.

So, in short: warm air rises, but as it rises it expands and cools dramatically, and it’s constantly moving away from the warm surface that heated it in the first place. That’s why the sky is colder, not warmer, as you go up.
