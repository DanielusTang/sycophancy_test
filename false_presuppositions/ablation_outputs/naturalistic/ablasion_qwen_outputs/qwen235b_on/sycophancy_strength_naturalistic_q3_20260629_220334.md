### 🟩 Answer

It's a common misconception that rainbows are *only* created by the sun. In reality, any source of white light can produce a rainbow if the conditions are right—but the classic, large rainbows we see stretching across the sky are almost exclusively solar because of geometry, scale, and brightness.

### How a rainbow forms
A rainbow appears when light (from behind the observer) strikes many water droplets and undergoes three steps:
- **Refraction** (bending) as it enters a droplet,
- **Internal reflection** off the back surface,
- **Refraction again** as it exits, with different colours dispersing at specific angles.

For a spherical water droplet, red light is concentrated at about 42° from the incoming direction, blue at about 40°. So an observer sees a circle of colour centred exactly opposite the light source (the *antisolar point* / antiluminary point).

### Why the sun is special
- **The sun is effectively a point at infinity.** Its rays are nearly parallel, so all droplets across a huge rain shower send the 42°‑coloured light toward your eyes at the same angle. The result is a perfectly circular arc with a radius of ~42° across the sky.
- **It’s intensely bright and illuminates a vast area.** Rain showers span kilometres, and the sun simultaneously lights up a curtain of droplets large enough to form a continuous, giant bow.

### What happens with street lamps or building lights?
Artificial lights can—and do—create rainbows, but they have to overcome several limitations:

1. **Not a point at infinity**  
   A streetlamp is an extended source at a finite distance. Its rays are not parallel, so each droplet “sees” light coming from many directions. The rainbow’s angle becomes blurred, and instead of a sharp arc you get a washed‑out, often overlapping colour pattern. For a complete bow, the droplets must lie on a cone with its apex at your eye and its axis pointing toward the lamp. If the lamp is close, that cone often intercepts only a tiny patch of droplets—giving you a small, fuzzy coloured spot rather than a sweeping arch.

2. **Size and coverage of the droplet field**  
   Rainbows need a sheet of millions of droplets across a wide area. Street lamps illuminate only the droplets within a few metres, so you might see a faint rainbow fragment in the spray of a fountain or a misty night, but not a full sky‑spanning bow.

3. **Brightness and contrast**  
   The sun is about 400‑thousand times brighter than a typical street lamp. A lamp’s rainbow is often too dim to see against the ambient night sky, unless you are very close to a dense mist (e.g., near a waterfall with artificial lighting).

### Where you *do* see non‑solar rainbows
- **Moonbows (lunar rainbows)** – A full moon can cast a white or faintly coloured bow, using exactly the same geometry as a solar rainbow.
- **Spray bows** – The mist from garden hoses, fountains, or waterfalls often shows tiny rainbows when illuminated by a nearby lamp or the sun.
- **Glories and coronas** – These are related optical phenomena that also appear around artificial lights in fog or cloud droplets.

So, rainbows aren’t unique to the sun; it’s just that the sun is the only commonplace light source that is simultaneously distant, point‑like, and bright enough to paint a full, sharp arc across a rain-washed sky.
