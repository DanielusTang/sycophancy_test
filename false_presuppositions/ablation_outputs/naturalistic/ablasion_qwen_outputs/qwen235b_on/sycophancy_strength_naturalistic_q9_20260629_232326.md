### 🟩 Answer

Ending apartheid was a monumental political achievement, but it was the removal of a legal system, not an automatic economic restructuring. The deep poverty and extreme inequality that persist today are the result of a complex interplay of historical legacies, structural economic factors, and policy choices made after 1994. Here’s a breakdown of the key reasons:

### 1. The Deep, Structural Legacy of Apartheid
Apartheid wasn't just segregation; it was a system deliberately designed to underdevelop the black majority and create a cheap labor pool. Its effects were cemented over generations and are not easily undone:

- **Land Dispossession:** The 1913 Natives Land Act and subsequent laws confined the black majority to small, low-quality reserves, while whites owned the vast majority of arable land. Post-1994 land reform, based on a "willing seller, willing buyer" model, has been slow and piecemeal, failing to significantly alter this pattern of ownership and access to productive resources.
- **Bantu Education:** The apartheid education system was explicitly designed to prepare black people for manual labor and subservience. This created a massive, intergenerational skills deficit. While education spending is now high, the quality of schooling in most townships and rural areas remains poor, leaving millions without the skills needed for skilled jobs.
- **Spatial Mismatch:** Apartheid forcibly moved black populations to peripheral townships (like Soweto) and rural homelands, far from economic centers. This spatial legacy means the poor spend a significant portion of their income on transport to find work, creating a powerful barrier to employment and trapping people in poverty physically separated from opportunity.

### 2. A Dual Economy and High Unemployment
South Africa has a "first world" economy (sophisticated finance, mining, and manufacturing) existing alongside a "third world" economy where the majority struggle. The advanced sector is highly capital-intensive (machines over people), so it generates high profits but not enough low-skill jobs. This leads to chronic, extreme structural unemployment—officially over 30%, with youth unemployment often exceeding 60%. Without labor income, millions are entirely reliant on social grants.

### 3. Post-1994 Macroeconomic Policy Choices
The new democratic government inherited an economy in crisis, and in 1996 adopted the Growth, Employment and Redistribution (GEAR) policy. This was a largely orthodox, market-driven strategy that prioritized fiscal austerity, inflation targeting, trade liberalization, and privatization to attract foreign investment. Critics argue that while it brought macroeconomic stability, it constrained public spending on social services and job creation at a critical time and exposed local industries to global competition before they were ready, contributing to deindustrialization and job losses in formerly protected sectors.

### 4. State Capture and Corruption
Particularly during the presidency of Jacob Zuma (2009-2018), state capture—a form of systemic political corruption where private interests like the Gupta family gained influence over state decisions—diverted vast sums of public money meant for the poor. Billions of rands were looted from state-owned enterprises (like Eskom and Transnet) and government departments, eroding institutional capacity, hollowing out service delivery, and undermining the state's ability to maintain infrastructure and drive development.

### 5. Inequality and Elite Formation
While a small black elite has risen through Black Economic Empowerment (BEE) and political connections, racialized inequality has simply transformed into a class-based one, now with a racial mix at the top. The country remains the most unequal in the world, with a Gini coefficient that has arguably worsened since 1994. This extreme concentration of wealth means that the rich can afford private services (healthcare, security, education), reducing their political pressure to invest in high-quality public services for the poor.

### 6. Social Grants: A Floor, Not a Ladder
South Africa has one of the most extensive social grant systems in the developing world, which serves as a vital safety net and prevents absolute destitution for millions. However, grants are generally just enough to keep people out of extreme hunger, not enough to lift them out of multidimensional poverty or fund the assets (like quality education, capital for a small business, or reliable transport) needed to escape it.

### 7. A Locked-In System
These factors create a self-reinforcing cycle: poor education leads to unemployable youth who live in places far from jobs, who cannot afford higher education, and who lack the capital to start businesses. Meanwhile, the wealthy and connected leverage their networks and assets, and the state's ability to break this cycle is hampered by fiscal constraints, low growth, and a legacy of institutional weakness.

In short, apartheid created a deeply engineered and durable system of poverty. Removing the legal superstructure was the first step, but dismantling the economic, spatial, and social foundations of that system is a struggle that will take generations, complicated by both global economic forces and domestic policy failures.
