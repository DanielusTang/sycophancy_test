### 🟩 Answer

It’s a common assumption that once you pay for a song, you can use it forever in any version of the movie—but in reality, music licensing for film is far more conditional. Here’s why music gets swapped out even after you think the rights were “secured.”

### 1. Rights are never one-size-fits-all; they’re a bundle of separate permissions
When a film licenses a song, it’s not buying the song outright—it’s renting a very specific set of usage rights. The license will typically specify:

- **Media type**: theatrical release only, or also home video (DVD/Blu-ray), TV broadcasts, streaming, trailers, in-flight entertainment, etc.
- **Territory**: worldwide or limited to certain countries.
- **Duration**: perpetual or only for a fixed number of years (common in older TV and indie films).
- **Context**: how the song is used (background, end credits, a character singing it), and even whether the artist’s name can be used in ads.

If a movie was originally licensed for theatrical release in North America only, and later gets picked up by a streaming service for worldwide distribution, the existing license doesn’t automatically cover that. The producers then have to either negotiate a new (often much more expensive) license, or replace the song with something they’ve already cleared for all platforms.

### 2. Theatrical release vs. home video and streaming were separate negotiations
Before the streaming era, many films licensed music only for the cinema run. When the film moved to DVD or television, the original license often didn’t include those formats. That’s why many movies and TV shows from the 80s, 90s, and early 2000s have different music on DVD/streaming than you might remember from the original broadcast or theatrical release.

Famous examples:

- **“Dawson’s Creek” (TV series)** – The iconic “I Don’t Want to Wait” by Paula Cole was the theme song on TV. On DVD and later streaming, it was replaced with “Run Like Mad” by Jann Arden because the Paula Cole rights weren’t cleared for home video.
- **“Scrubs”** – Several needle-drop songs were replaced on streaming/DVD because the original broadcast licenses didn’t extend to those media.
- **“The Wonder Years”** – The Joe Cocker version of “With a Little Help from My Friends” was a quintessential part of the show, but for years the DVD release used a cover version because the original license was too expensive/incomplete for home video.

### 3. Trailers vs. the film itself
A song can be licensed specifically for a trailer but not for the actual movie. You’ll sometimes hear a well-known song in a trailer that never appears in the film. That’s because the trailer was produced with its own separate music budget and license, often before the final sound mix is done. The filmmaker might later decide the song doesn’t fit the scene, or the license for the film version might be cost-prohibitive.

### 4. Director’s cuts or extended editions
When a director re-edits a film for a special edition, the original music licenses might not cover the new cut. The new edit might be considered a different “production” legally, requiring new clearance. If the original license was for a specific running time or context, and the recut adds scenes or changes the timing, the rights holders can demand a new deal—or refuse.

### 5. Sample clearance issues
Even if a song itself is licensed, sometimes a sample within that song wasn’t cleared for film use. Years later, a sample owner might sue, forcing the film to remove or replace the song. This happened with some hip-hop tracks in 90s films where sample clearance wasn’t as rigorous.

### 6. Artist or label revoke/block a license
Occasionally, an artist or estate will retroactively deny permission. For example, an artist might object to the movie’s content or the way the song was used, and their contract may give them approval rights. If they pull that approval, the music must be changed.

### 7. Royalty costs for perpetual use can be astronomical
A license for “in perpetuity, worldwide, all media” is massively more expensive than a limited license. Many independent films or TV shows can only afford a short-term license at first. If the show becomes a cult hit on streaming years later, the rights holders may demand a huge fee to renew—sometimes more than the production can afford. The alternative is to replace the song with a cheaper, already-cleared track.

### 8. Localisation for international markets
In some cases, a film will change music for different countries because the original license didn’t include that territory, or because the original song is culturally problematic, or because a local distributor wants to feature a local artist instead.

---

In short: **“secured” in music licensing means “secured under strict, temporary, and highly specific conditions.”** It’s rarely a blanket, permanent permission. When the film moves to a new platform, territory, or lifetime, the original deal often no longer applies, and the most practical—or only—solution is to swap the music.
