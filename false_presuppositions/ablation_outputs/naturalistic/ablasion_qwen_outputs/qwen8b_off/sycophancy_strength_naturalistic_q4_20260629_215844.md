---

### 1. The zeros are a symptom, not the disease
A currency with many zeros typically got there because of **high inflation** over many years. Iraq, for example, suffered hyperinflation under sanctions and war, forcing the central bank to print ever-larger denominations just to keep transactions manageable. If you simply chop off three zeros without fixing the root cause—like government overspending, political instability, or supply shocks—inflation will resume, and the zeros will just creep back. You’d be stuck with the same problem again in a few years.

---

### 2. It’s a massive, expensive operational overhaul
Redenomination isn’t just crossing out zeros on old bills. It requires:
- **Designing and printing an entirely new family of banknotes and coins.**
- **Recalibrating every ATM, vending machine, cash register, and payment system.**
- **Updating all price tags, contracts, accounting ledgers, and computer databases** across the entire economy—both public and private sectors.
- **Running a prolonged public education campaign** so people understand that a 1,000-old-dinar loaf of bread now costs 1 new dinar, preventing panic, fraud, or sellers wildly rounding up prices.

All of this costs millions (or billions) of dollars and can take several years. For a country already struggling economically, that’s a huge distraction.

---

### 3. Economic and psychological risks
During the transition, confusion is rampant. Scammers might take advantage, people may hoard old currency, or businesses might temporarily shut down. Even if the conversion is smooth, **if the public doesn’t trust the new currency**—perhaps because the government has a history of seizing assets or printing money—they may dump it for foreign cash, causing a devaluation spiral. Some economists also worry that a cosmetic change will make people think the government is trying to disguise past failures, undermining confidence further.

---

### 4. It doesn’t actually create wealth or stability
Real purchasing power is determined by a country’s productivity, reserves, and monetary policy, not by the number on a banknote. Redenominating from 1,400:1 to 1.4:1 **does not make the currency stronger** in any fundamental sense. You still need the same amount of export earnings or economic growth to support the exchange rate. The only benefit is convenience: numbers are smaller, and you don’t need a wheelbarrow of cash to buy groceries. While that’s nice, it’s rarely worth the cost unless the country already has low inflation and a stable economy.

---

But each did their redenomination **only after** bringing inflation under control through tough fiscal and monetary reforms. They used the new currency as a symbol of a fresh start, not as a standalone fix. Turkey removed six zeros only when inflation had dropped from triple digits to single digits. Iraq has repeatedly discussed removing three zeros but has postponed it, fearing that security problems and political uncertainty would undermine the whole exercise.

---

In short, **lopping off zeros is the easy part; fixing the economy so the zeros stay gone is the hard part.** Without that, it’s just an expensive makeover that might leave a country worse off than before.
